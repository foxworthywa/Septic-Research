# Outreach Messaging Review — October 2026

*What the evidence supports saying in the Eastern Shore Post series, what to stop saying, and how to read VDH's "we're not seeing it."*

*EI Climate Collaborative ESVA Septic Project. Prepared for Alex Foxworthy and the project team, October 2026. Every number in Section 2 and Appendix A has been checked against a primary source; where it hasn't, it says so.*

---

## Bottom line

1. **Your read on ecology is right.** After checking the key sources, there is **no published evidence that septic systems are causing measurable ecological harm on the Eastern Shore.**
   - The paper the project leaned on most, Mitchell et al. (2021), says close to the opposite of what we had attributed to it.
   - The Shore studies that *do* exist point to agriculture as the main nitrogen source.
   - VDH's monitoring shows the Shore's shellfish waters are among the cleanest in Virginia.
2. **That is not the same as "septic is harmless."** It means the local effect is **unmeasured**, and so far it does not show up in the data VDH collects. That makes it a research question, not an outreach message.
3. **The productive message is the one VDH asked for, and it is well supported:**
   - find out whether you have a septic system;
   - know who to call;
   - pump it out regularly. That protects an expensive piece of your house and your family's water, and the law requires it.
4. **On health, stay with well testing, infants and nitrate, and flooded wells.** All three are solid and actionable. Drop the cancer, thyroid, chronic-disease and lead-paint comparisons.
5. **Several numbers circulating in project materials are wrong.** The two most important:
   - **"43% of Shore wells contaminated."** The 43% is samples above a *sodium* guidance level.
   - **Chincoteague "losing its wells to septic."** No source documents this; the island's water was brackish.

The two articles and the resource guide have been rewritten on this basis:

- `outreach/article_part1_septic_basics.md`
- `outreach/article_part2_well_testing.md`
- `outreach/resident_resources_guide.md`

## 1. "VDH isn't seeing it." How much is VDH actually measuring?

This was your open question. The answer differs a lot by topic.

| Topic | Who measures it, and how | How much weight "not seeing it" carries |
|---|---|---|
| **Shellfish waters (bacteria)** | VDH Division of Shellfish Safety. **About 590 active Shore stations, each sampled at least six times a year.** Every Shore growing area has a current property-by-property shoreline survey (2018–2026). VDH is starting to use a human-specific DNA marker (HF183). | **High.** If septic systems were widely contaminating shellfish waters, this system would show it. By our analysis of VDH's public map data, about **1% of the Shore's classified shellfish acreage is condemned, against about 6% statewide.** Problems are local: Bayside creek headwaters, harbors and mandatory marina/outfall buffers, closures after heavy rain, and Chincoteague's cesspools. VDH's 2026 notices also appear to reopen several large, formerly condemned sections (partly a remapping effect, so treat this as "likely," not proven). |
| **Swimming beaches** | VDH, weekly in summer at four Shore beaches | **Moderate.** Occasional advisories (e.g., Cape Charles, August 2025). Sources are not identified. |
| **Illness from drinking water** | Only through disease reporting | **Low for private wells.** Surveillance catches *outbreaks*. A stomach bug from one family's well is rarely lab-tested or traced to the water (CDC: outbreak data "likely underestimate actual occurrence"). Not seeing illness neither confirms nor rules out sporadic household cases. That is an argument for routine testing, not for alarm. |
| **Private well water** | Nobody routinely; it's the owner's job | The best local data are volunteer Extension clinics: **340 samples, 2014–17. Coliform 27%, *E. coli* 4%, nitrate over the limit in fewer than 5%.** Serious problems are the exception; bacteria are the common issue. |
| **Septic systems themselves** | VDH, partly | **Poorly, and VDH says so.** Its database holds records for only about **37%** of the Shore's estimated systems. Only **436** pump-outs were reported on the Shore between July 2023 and September 2025. It handles about **90 repair permits a year**. Shoreline surveys are visual. **This is the real blind spot, and it's what VDH is asking for help with.** |
| **Where creek nitrogen and bacteria come from** | Nobody, with current methods | **Unknown.** The only Shore source tracking (2006–08 TMDLs) used a method EPA called "experimental." It attributed 24–60% of creek bacteria to "human" sources, a category that also includes boats and sewer lines. A 2017 model-based TMDL put human sources at ~1–2% and wildlife at 61–95%. Studies of Shore nitrogen point to fertilizer first. One UVA thesis on the seaside lagoons concluded "human waste was a minor constituent." |

**Reading it together:**

- VDH's statement about shellfish rests on intensive, real monitoring and should be taken at face value.
- Its statement about public health rests on surveillance that is weak for private wells. That is a reason to promote testing, not to suggest VDH is missing an epidemic. The well-clinic data agree that serious problems are uncommon.
- The genuinely unmeasured thing is the **septic systems themselves**: where they are, whether they're maintained, and how they're aging as water tables rise. That is exactly what the pump-out program, and this project, are trying to fix. It is a much better story for us than "septic is hurting the bay."

## 2. What to say: the core messages

These map one-to-one onto what VDH asked for, and every supporting fact traces to a primary source (Appendix A).

### A. "Do you have a septic system?"

- **Most Shore homes do.** Public sewer exists in:
  - Cape Charles (now Virginia American Water);
  - Tangier;
  - the parts of Nassawadox, Exmore, Keller, Onley, Onancock, Accomac and Wachapreague served by HRSD's Route 13 line;
  - a few developments.

  Chincoteague is mostly septic, with some cesspools.
- **If you have a well or no sewer charge on your bill, you're almost certainly on septic.**
- **The health district may have your permit and a drawing.** Many older systems have no record, so "no record" doesn't mean "no system."
- **Alternative systems carry extra obligations.** These are the systems with a control panel, alarm, pump or mound, and they need an annual operator inspection by law.

### B. "Pumping pays." Financial self-interest, the strongest honest motivator

- **What VDH says:** unpumped solids and grease reaching the drainfield "can cause the absorption area to fail."
- **What it costs (VDH figures):**
  - routine pump-out: about **$430** on average, and higher on the Shore because septage is trucked to Maryland;
  - failed conventional system: about **$19,650**;
  - alternative system: about **$34,800**.
- **When it comes up anyway:**
  - The health district asks for **pump-out documentation for building permits.**
  - A documented system makes a **sale** easier.
- **The honest caveat (include it, because it builds trust):** pumping won't fix a drainfield sitting in high groundwater. It prevents the most common *avoidable* failure.

### C. "It's also the law, and VDH would rather remind than fine"

- **The requirement:** under state law, septic tanks in Chesapeake Bay Preservation Areas must be pumped (or inspected and certified) **at least every five years**. VDH has enforced this on the Shore since **July 1, 2023**.
- **The letters:** VDH began mailing reminders in 2024. About **one in four** of the first ~2,000 Northampton letters got a response. *The letter is a reminder, not a fine.*
- **Two practical asks:**
  - ask the hauler to report the pump-out to VDH (they often don't unless asked);
  - keep the receipt.
- **Other rules owners should know:**
  - Sewage on the ground or in a ditch is a violation.
  - Repairs need a VDH permit, and **the permit fee is waived** for failing systems.
  - Low-income owners can get a **free system design.**
- **Why the rule exists:** Virginia counts pump-outs toward its Chesapeake Bay cleanup. That is one honest ecological sentence.

### D. "Test your well." The health message

- **Nobody else tests your well.** Virginia doesn't require it, and the federal law doesn't apply to private wells.
- **What Shore clinics found:** about **one sample in four** had coliform bacteria, a sign surface water may be getting in. **4%** had *E. coli*. **Nitrate over the limit was uncommon.**
- **Most Shore wells are deep and well protected.** The weak points are the well itself (cap, casing, seal) and what's near it, **including the septic system.** Required setbacks are 50 ft from a well to a tank, and 50–100 ft to a drainfield.
- **When to test:** every year for bacteria and nitrate; **after a flood** (VDH: assume a submerged well is contaminated); **before a baby comes home.**
- **Infants:** nitrate in formula is the one well-established infant risk. **Boiling concentrates nitrate.** Breastfed babies aren't at risk.

### E. "Who to call"

- **Septic records, requirements, failing systems, well questions:** Eastern Shore Health District, Environmental Health, **(757) 787-5886**.
- **Help paying:** A-NPDC, **(757) 787-2936**. The last DEQ round closed in August 2025 and was Bayside only, so the message is "call to get on the list."
- **Well testing:** Extension, Accomack (757) 787-1361 and Northampton (757) 678-7946. Kits are $70; the annual clinic was August 11, 2026.

### Ready-to-use sentences

- "If you don't pay a sewer bill, there's a small wastewater treatment plant buried in your yard."
- "A pump-out every three to five years is the cheapest insurance you can buy for one of the most expensive parts of your house."
- "That letter from the Health Department wasn't a fine. It was a reminder."
- "Nobody tests your well but you."
- "Boiling kills bacteria. It does not remove nitrate; it concentrates it."
- "The Shore's shellfish waters are tested at hundreds of stations several times a year, and the great majority are open for harvest. Keeping septic systems working is one of the ordinary ways we keep it that way."

| 2 | "Your well draws from the same shallow aquifer your septic drains into" | Most Shore household wells are deep (median ~160–190 ft) in confined aquifers. ## 4. Where the ecological story stands

**What we can honestly say:**

> The Shore's creeks get most of their fresh water from groundwater, and a conventional septic system passes most of its nitrogen into groundwater. Nobody has yet measured how much of the nitrogen or bacteria in Shore waters comes from septic systems rather than farms, wildlife or other sources. The studies that exist point to agriculture first. VDH monitors shellfish waters closely, and the Shore's are among the cleanest in Virginia. Keeping septic systems working is part of keeping it that way. As water tables rise, that will take more attention, not less.

For a newspaper, use **one or two sentences** of that at most. Part 1 uses two:

- Virginia counts pump-outs toward Bay cleanup.
- The shellfish waters are monitored and in good shape.

**What would change the answer.** These are good ESCC capstones, and a null result is publishable and useful to VDH:

1. **Overlay** VDH shellfish closure areas and failing stations against septic density and VDH repair-permit locations. Both are public, it has never been done, and it's semester-scale.
2. **Human-marker sampling** (HF183) in the chronically failing Bayside creek headwaters (Nassawadox/Westerhouse, Onancock, Pungoteague, Hungars), coordinated with VDH's new HF183 work.
3. **Groundwater tracers** (nitrate isotopes plus sucralose/acesulfame) at the shoreline below septic clusters versus below farm fields.

**The forward-looking story that *is* defensible is rising groundwater.** The mechanism is well established, and it has been measured in Rhode Island and North Carolina. Project modeling projects it on the Shore. It belongs in a later piece once the project's modeling (Hesamfar et al.) is published, or when VDH's regulation review produces a proposal. It should be framed as a planning issue for homeowners and the counties, not as current harm.

## 6. Questions to settle with VDH and partners before publishing

1. **Pump-out coverage.** Does the 5-year requirement apply on the **Seaside** and inside **incorporated towns**?
   - Accomack's ordinance covers the Seaside but excludes towns.
   - Northampton treats the whole county as covered.
   - VDH's 2025 report says only Bay-watershed properties must pump out.

   We need one sentence VDH is happy to see printed.
2. **Alternatives to pumping.** Are effluent filters or inspection certificates accepted on the Shore?
3. **Reporting.** What should homeowners do to make sure a pump-out is on record: ask the hauler, or send the receipt to ESHD, and how?
4. **Phone number.** Which number they want printed (we use 757-787-5886, Environmental Health).
5. **Framing.** Are they comfortable with "the letter wasn't a fine," and with naming the **Hardship Guidelines** (free design) in print?
6. **Shellfish wording.** Are they comfortable with the Part 1 sentence ("nearly 600 stations… great majority open")? Do they want to say anything about the new HF183 testing?
7. **A-NPDC.** Is a new round expected in 2026–27, and will it be Bayside only?
8. **Extension.** The 2027 clinic dates, and whether fee assistance is available.
9. **ESCC.** Which contact to publish, and whether any free screening events are confirmed. Don't print "free testing events" until they are.

## 8. Why these errors persisted, and a simple fix

Most of the problems share one origin. Numbers and attributions produced during AI-assisted research drafting entered project documents without being checked against the primary source. Then they were copied from document to document:

- the 43% figure;
- Chincoteague;
- the thyroid claim;
- the Mitchell overlap;
- the 20–40% share;
- the town list.

When the poster review caught some of them in July, the fixes never propagated back to the synthesis and articles. Your own catch on the 80% figure, which you traced to the 2010 Water Supply Plan, is the model to follow.

**The fix:** Appendix A below is a single sheet of canonical numbers, each with its primary source. **If a number isn't on the sheet, it doesn't go in a public piece until someone adds it with a source.** When a number changes, change it on the sheet first.
