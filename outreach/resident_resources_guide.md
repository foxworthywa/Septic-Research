# Eastern Shore of Virginia: Septic & Well Resources for Residents

*A practical directory of where to go for help with septic systems and private wells in Accomack and Northampton Counties.*

*Compiled for the EI Climate Collaborative ESVA Septic Project. Companion to the Eastern Shore Post article series. Last researched: June 2026. **Phone numbers, program details, and eligibility rules change — verify with the organization before relying on any specific detail.***

---

## How to use this guide

If you own a home on the Eastern Shore with a well and a septic system, this guide tells you who to call to (1) find out what's in your water, (2) keep your septic system healthy, (3) find out whether your system is failing, and (4) get financial help if it needs repair or replacement.

You don't need to do everything at once. If you do only one thing, **get your well water tested** — it's cheap, it's the step that tells you whether anything else is urgent, and it's covered in Section 1.

Each entry below tells you what the resource is, roughly what it costs, who qualifies, and how to reach them. Costs and eligibility change, so the phone numbers are the most important part — call and ask.

## 1. Getting your well water tested

Most Eastern Shore wells have never been tested, and Virginia does not require it. Testing is the single most useful thing a well owner can do, because contaminated water looks and tastes exactly like clean water — the only way to know is to test. There are three main paths.

### Option A — Virginia Household Water Quality Program (best value, most thorough)

This is the program to start with for most residents. Run by Virginia Tech and Virginia Cooperative Extension since 1989, it holds local "drinking water clinics" through county Extension offices.

- **What you get:** analysis for 28 substances at once, including **bacteria (total coliform and E. coli), nitrate, lead, arsenic, copper, iron, hardness, pH**, and more.
- **Cost:** about **$70 per sample kit** — and grant funding can bring it down to about **$10** for families who need the assistance. (The same panel at a private commercial lab would cost more than $300.)
- **How it works:** you pick up a sample kit at a scheduled clinic, collect water at home following the instructions, return the kit, and get results by mail or email — plus a meeting to help you interpret them — about 4–5 weeks later.
- **Clinics run February through November.** Dates vary; you have to ask your local Extension office when the next Eastern Shore clinic is scheduled.
- **How to reach them:**
  - **Accomack County Extension:** 23185 Front Street, Accomac, VA — **(757) 787-1361** — vceaccomack@vt.edu
  - **Northampton County Extension:** **(757) 678-7946** — amandalh@vt.edu
  - Program statewide: Virginia Household Water Quality Program, <https://www.wellwater.bse.vt.edu/>

### Option B — Eastern Shore Health District (Virginia Department of Health)

VDH provides private well water testing information and, in some situations, testing support — particularly when there is a health concern (a new baby, a suspected contamination event, illness in the household). Start here if you have a specific health worry rather than a general "I've never tested" situation.

- **Eastern Shore Health District:** **(757) 787-5886** (Accomac) / **(757) 442-6228** (Nassawadox)
- VDH private well water testing: <https://www.vdh.virginia.gov/environmental-health/water-testing/>

### Option C — Private certified laboratory

If you need results fast (for example, for a real estate transaction) or want a specific single test, you can use a state-certified commercial lab directly. This is the most expensive route. Ask the lab for the price of the specific panel you need (a basic bacteria + nitrate test is far cheaper than the full panel).

- A list of certified labs is available through VDH's Division of Consolidated Laboratory Services.

### Free testing events through the project

The EI Climate Collaborative Septic Project and Eastern Shore Community College run **free well-water testing events** at community gatherings — Earth Day in April, SepticSmart Week in September, and other events through the year. These are a no-cost way to get screened for the most important contaminants (nitrate and bacteria) and to ask questions in person. Watch the Eastern Shore Post and ESCC announcements, or contact the project team (Section 5).

## 2. Getting your septic tank pumped out

A pump-out every 3–5 years is the cheapest, most effective thing you can do to keep a septic system healthy and avoid a far more expensive failure. In fact, **Accomack County regulations require most on-site systems to be pumped out at least once every five years.**

### Free and discounted pump-outs — the Eastern Shore Septic Pump-Out Program

This is the program most residents should know about. It is administered by the **Accomack-Northampton Planning District Commission (A-NPDC)** and funded primarily by the Virginia Department of Environmental Quality as part of cleaning up the Chesapeake Bay.

- **What it covers:** depending on your income and location, the program can pay for a **free or discounted pump-out, and in some cases repair or even full replacement** of a failing system.
- **Who qualifies:**
  - Homeowners (and in some cases landlords) in Accomack or Northampton County.
  - The strongest benefit (up to 100% covered) is for low- and moderate-income households — generally those below about 80% of area median income (roughly $40,000/year, but check current figures).
  - **Higher incomes still qualify for help.** Even households above 120% of county median income are eligible for roughly a 50% cost-share on a pump-out, and do not have to document their income for that level.
  - The cost-share works on an **income-based sliding scale** — the less you make, the more is covered.
- **Important location note:** because much of the funding comes from Chesapeake Bay cleanup money, the program has historically prioritized properties in the **Chesapeake Bay watershed (the Bayside)**. Seaside residents should still call and ask — funding sources change, and other programs may apply.
- **Funding is limited and first-come, first-served.** Apply early in the cycle.
- **How to reach them:** **A-NPDC at (757) 787-2936** (Accomac office), or <https://www.esvaplan.org/>. You can also ask **Accomack County Department of Environmental Programs** or the **Northampton County housing office** to point you to the current application.

### Paying out of pocket — hiring a licensed pumper

If you don't qualify for assistance or don't want to wait, a routine pump-out from a licensed septic hauler runs roughly **$300–$500** for a typical tank. Several licensed onsite/septic businesses operate on the Shore (for example, locally based wastewater companies that also do installation and repair). Ask for a licensed operator, and ask them to note the tank's condition while they're there — a good pumper will tell you whether you have a tank problem or a drainfield problem (see Section 3).

> **Why this matters:** a roughly $400 pump-out, done on schedule, can prevent a $20,000–$40,000 system replacement on Shore terrain where high water tables force expensive engineered systems. It is the best return on investment in this entire guide.

## 3. Getting your septic system inspected or evaluated

If you suspect a problem — slow drains, sewage smell, soggy ground over the drainfield, backups after rain — or you just want to know the condition of an older system, you have a few options.

### Warning signs that mean "call someone now"

- Sewage odor indoors or outdoors, especially after rain
- Standing water or unusually green, spongy grass over the drainfield
- Slow drains or toilets backing up
- Gurgling in the plumbing
- Well water that suddenly tests positive for bacteria or higher nitrate

### Who can evaluate the system

- **Licensed septic pumper/hauler (quickest, cheapest first look).** When they pump the tank, a good operator can tell you whether the tank itself is failing or whether the problem is in the drainfield. This is often the fastest way to find out what kind of problem you have.
- **Authorized Onsite Soil Evaluator (AOSE) / Private Onsite Soil Evaluator (OSE).** For anything beyond a routine pump-out — a suspected drainfield failure, a repair, or a planned replacement — you'll need a licensed soil evaluator. **Each Eastern Shore Health District office keeps a list of private OSEs** who can evaluate your existing system, determine what repairs or upgrades are needed, and prepare the permit package that VDH requires before any repair or new installation. Call the health district (Section 5) and ask for the current OSE list.
- **A licensed onsite system installer/contractor.** For repairs and replacements, a licensed installer works from the OSE's evaluation and the VDH permit.

### The tank-failure vs. drainfield-failure distinction (important)

Not all "septic failures" are the same, and they cost very different amounts to fix. A **tank problem** (cracked tank, broken baffle, full of solids) is often relatively inexpensive. A **drainfield problem** (the soil can no longer absorb the water — common on the Shore as the water table rises) is far more expensive and may require an engineered or alternative system. Ask whoever evaluates your system which one you have, because it determines everything about cost and which assistance programs fit.
