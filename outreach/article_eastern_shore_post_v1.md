## Article body

**What's underground at your house ends up in Hog Island Bay**

Drive out to Willis Wharf at dawn and you'll see boats heading out to the clam grounds. Stop at any restaurant in Cape Charles and the half-shells on the menu probably came out of an Accomack or Northampton creek the day before. Walk the seaside flats at low tide and, in the right places, you'll find something most of the East Coast lost a hundred years ago and only the Eastern Shore has gotten back: living eelgrass meadows, with bay scallops in them.

That's not a tourism brochure. That's an industry. Virginia shellfish farmers brought in about $81 million in direct sales last year, more than half again what they earned just six years ago, and roughly 99 percent of the state's hard-clam harvest comes off these two counties. More than 700 people on the Shore make their living from it. The seaside seagrass restoration — about 10,000 acres of eelgrass replanted in South Bay, Cobb Bay, Hog Island Bay, and Spider Crab Bay — is the largest successful seagrass comeback in the world. Bay scallops, gone from these waters since 1933, are back and breeding because of it.

All of that depends on clean water in the creeks. And clean water in the creeks depends, more than most folks realize, on what's happening underground at your house.

The Eastern Shore sits on a single shallow aquifer — the same water your well draws from is the same water your drainfield empties into. About eighty percent of the water flowing through Accomack County's tidal creeks comes up out of the ground, not down out of the sky. So when a septic system isn't working right, what leaks out doesn't stay at your property. It moves through the sand and the gravel and a few months or a few years later it shows up in a creek mouth, in an oyster lease, in a clam bed, or in a brown haze of algae over an eelgrass meadow.

Researchers at the Virginia Institute of Marine Science, the University of Virginia, the Virginia Coast Reserve, and Eastern Shore Community College have spent the last few years putting numbers on this. The picture is consistent. Where there's a lot of older septic on the Shore, there's more nitrogen in the creeks, more bacteria after rainstorms, more closed shellfish days, and less margin of safety for the eelgrass meadow that holds the whole seaside system together. The closure maps and the failing-septic maps line up almost exactly — at Oyster, at Wachapreague, at Quinby, at Saxis, at Chincoteague, at Cape Charles. The Virginia Department of Health watches its shellfish water-quality numbers rise from about seven percent over the limit on dry days to nearly forty percent over the limit after an inch of rain. Headwater creeks can be closed sixty days a year in a wet year.

This isn't a story about blame. Most Shore septic systems were installed decades ago, when the groundwater was lower, the storms were less intense, and the rules were less demanding. They worked fine then. They're working less well now, mostly through no fault of the homeowner. Sea level is rising on the Shore faster than almost anywhere on the East Coast, and as the water table comes up, the soil layer that's supposed to clean the wastewater before it reaches groundwater gets thinner and thinner. A drainfield that was perfectly compliant in 1985 may be marginal today and failing by 2040.

That's the structural part of the story, and it's not something any one household can fix on its own. What you *can* do is make your own system last longer, leak less, and contribute less to the problem in the meantime. The good news is that the cheapest, easiest things to do are also the most effective. And several of them are free.

The single most important thing is a regular tank pump-out. Every three to five years, depending on household size. A pump-out costs about $400 if you pay out of pocket, and it can prevent a $20,000 to $40,000 system replacement on Shore terrain where engineered systems are the only option. The Accomack–Northampton Planning District Commission has run programs that cover pump-outs at no cost to qualifying low- and moderate-income households. Most of the people who could use these programs don't know they exist. The Virginia Department of Health's Eastern Shore office sends letters about pump-outs every year — only about one in four homeowners writes back. If you got one of those letters and tossed it, dig it back out.

The second is what you put in the system. Septic installers on the Shore say two things kill systems: age and what gets flushed. Wet wipes — even the ones labeled "flushable" — grease and cooking oil, paint, harsh chemicals, and excessive amounts of any cleaner all shorten system life and can directly cause the failures that contaminate creeks. The rule of thumb is the three P's: pee, poop, and (toilet) paper. Everything else goes in the trash.

The third is well water testing. Most Shore wells have never been tested, because Virginia does not require it. Free or low-cost nitrate and bacteria tests show up at community events on the Shore — Earth Day, SepticSmart Week in September, and the local well-testing days run through Eastern Shore Community College and the health district. A test result is more motivating than any newspaper article. If you've never had your water tested, this is the year.

The fourth is helping water move where it's supposed to. Drainage ditches that fill up with leaves and limbs back water onto drainfields, push the water table higher, and push more systems toward failure. Targeted ditch maintenance can reduce rainfall-driven flooding by as much as ten percent in some neighborhoods. The Shore's tradition of community work days is exactly the right tool for this; the project team is helping organize a few of them in 2026 and would welcome volunteers.

And finally — if a sewer line ever reaches your property as part of the Hampton Roads Sanitation District's Route 13 expansion through Nassawadox, Exmore, Onley, Onancock, Accomac, and Wachapreague, take it. The hookup costs are real, but the long-term savings and the protection it gives the creeks downstream are bigger. Programs to help cover hookup costs are part of the larger conversation the planning district commission is having with the state.

None of this is about scaring anyone. The Shore has gotten back things — the seagrass, the scallops, the clam grounds — that other coastal communities have lost forever. That recovery is fragile, and it depends on a fragile balance underground. A $400 pump-out, a free water test, and the three P's at the toilet are the kind of small, ordinary things that, multiplied across 18,000 households, decide whether the Shore keeps what it has built back.

The clams in Willis Wharf and the seagrass in Hog Island Bay are not abstractions. They are jobs and food and a place worth living in. They begin with what is underground at your house.

## Editor's note / sidebar — "What you can do today"

**1. Pump out your tank.** Every 3–5 years for most households. Costs about $400 if you pay yourself; free for qualifying low- and moderate-income households through the Accomack–Northampton Planning District Commission. A $400 pump-out can prevent a $20,000–$40,000 system replacement.

**2. Get your well water tested.** Most Shore wells have never been tested. Free testing days happen on Earth Day (April 25 this year), during SepticSmart Week in September, and through Eastern Shore Community College student programs. Walk in with a clean container and walk out with your nitrate and bacteria numbers.

**3. Mind the three P's at the toilet.** Pee, poop, and toilet paper only. No "flushable" wipes — they aren't. No grease, no paint, no harsh chemicals down the drain in any quantity.

**4. Apply for repair help if you need it.** Virginia's Onsite Sewage Indemnification Fund covers households at up to twice the federal poverty level. Most homeowners who qualify don't know it exists. The planning district commission and the health district can help you fill out the paperwork at the kitchen table.

**5. Keep your ditches clear.** A blocked ditch backs water onto your drainfield. Targeted ditch maintenance days are being organized for 2026; volunteers welcome.

**6. If sewer comes to you, take it.** The Hampton Roads Sanitation District is extending sewer lines through seven Shore towns. If your property is on the line, the long-term protection is worth the hookup cost. Help with hookup fees may be available — ask.

## Editor's note / sidebar — "Where to call"

- **Eastern Shore Health District (Virginia Department of Health):** 757-787-5886 — well water testing, septic permits, repair-program information.
- **Accomack–Northampton Planning District Commission:** the office in Accomac handles the pump-out and septic repair assistance programs and can help with applications.
- **Eastern Shore Community College — Smart Septic Project:** Dr. Alex Foxworthy and the student team are running well water testing events and community outreach as part of the UVA Climate Collaborative project. ESCC main office, Melfa.
- **For shellfish closure questions:** VDH Division of Shellfish Safety publishes the closure status for every classified water on the Shore at the VDH environmental health website.
