## Article body

**Is your home on septic? Here's what the Health Department wants you to know**

If you live on the Eastern Shore and you don't pay a sewer bill, there is a small wastewater treatment system buried in your yard. Most of us never think about it until something goes wrong: a smell after a heavy rain, a toilet that won't drain, a soggy patch of lawn. By then the cheap fix has usually turned into an expensive one.

The Eastern Shore Health District, the local office of the Virginia Department of Health, would like to change that. Since the summer of 2023, the health district, rather than the counties, has been responsible for making sure septic tanks on the Shore get pumped on schedule [1]. In 2024 it began mailing reminder letters, starting with about 2,000 homes in Northampton County. About one in four wrote back [1].

If you got one of those letters and set it aside, it wasn't a bill and it wasn't a fine. It was a reminder. Health district staff have said they would rather help people keep their systems working than penalize anyone. What they want is for every homeowner on the Shore to know three things: whether they have a septic system, who to call, and what to do to keep it working.

**First: do you have one?**

Probably. Outside Cape Charles, Tangier, and the parts of Nassawadox, Exmore, Keller, Onley, Onancock, Accomac and Wachapreague served by the new Route 13 sewer line, nearly every home on the Shore is on septic [2]. So is most of Chincoteague. If you have a private well, or your utility bill has no sewer charge, you almost certainly do.

If you're not sure where the tank is, look for round or square lids or plastic risers a few feet from the house, or a white pipe sticking up out of the lawn. The health district may have a permit drawing that shows the location; call with your address or tax map number. Many older systems predate the records, so "no record" doesn't mean "no system." A licensed pumper can find the tank.

It's also worth knowing what kind you have. Many newer and repaired systems on the Shore are "alternative" systems designed for wet ground: the kind with a control panel, an alarm box, a pump, or a raised mound. They work well, but they come with an extra obligation. A licensed operator has to inspect them every year [3].

**Second: why pumping pays**

A septic system has two parts:

- **The tank** lets solids sink and grease float.
- **The drainfield**, the pipes and soil out in the yard, does most of the actual cleaning of the water.

The tank is cheap. The drainfield is not.

Over the years, sludge and grease build up in the tank. If nobody pumps them out, they eventually wash into the drainfield and clog the soil. In its 2025 report to the General Assembly, VDH put it plainly: those solids and grease "can cause the absorption area to fail" [1].

Here's the arithmetic:

- **A routine pump-out** averages about $430 in Virginia [4]. It can run higher on the Shore, where most of what's pumped is trucked to Maryland for disposal [1].
- **Repairing or replacing a failed conventional system** averaged nearly $20,000 in VDH's statewide assistance programs [4].
- **An engineered or alternative system**, often the only option on our low, wet lots, averaged about $35,000 [4].

A pump-out every three to five years is about the cheapest insurance you can buy for one of the most expensive parts of your house.

It won't prevent every problem. A drainfield sitting in high groundwater has troubles a pump truck can't fix. But pumping prevents the most common failure that is completely avoidable.

There are other practical reasons, too:

- **Building or renovating:** when you add on or renovate, the health district asks for proof of a recent pump-out as part of approving the building permit [1].
- **Selling:** buyers and inspectors look at the septic system. A system with receipts is an easier sale than one nobody has opened in twenty years.

**Third: it's also the law**

Many Shore homeowners don't realize that state law requires septic tanks in Chesapeake Bay Preservation Areas to be pumped out at least once every five years [5]. On the Shore that covers most properties, and the health district can tell you whether it applies at your address. The rule comes from the Chesapeake Bay Preservation Act. Its purpose is to keep systems working, and Virginia counts each reported pump-out toward its Chesapeake Bay cleanup goals [1].

Two practical tips:

- When your tank is pumped, **ask the company to report it to VDH.** Haulers are required to, but on the Shore many reports are only filed after an owner gets a letter [1].
- **Keep the receipt.**

If you ever see sewage on the ground or in a ditch, that's both a health hazard and a violation of state rules [6]. Don't wait it out; call the health district. Repairs require a VDH permit, but the permit fee is waived for failing systems [6], and homeowners with low incomes can get the system design done for free [7].

**Who to call**

**Start with the Eastern Shore Health District's environmental health office at (757) 787-5886** [8]. Staff there can:

- look up your records;
- tell you what's required at your address;
- give you a list of licensed pumpers;
- point you toward help.

**For help paying, call the Accomack-Northampton Planning District Commission at (757) 787-2936.** The commission has run several rounds of state-funded help with pump-outs, repairs and replacements. The most recent round closed in 2025, so call to get on the list for the next one [7]. Other sources of help are in the sidebar.

**Your family comes first**

A working septic system protects the people who live closest to it, your own family most of all. Sewage on the surface is a direct health risk to children and pets, and a failing system can contaminate nearby wells, including your own. If you have a well, the natural partner to a pump-out is a well test. Next week's article covers how to get one and what the results mean.

None of this is complicated:

- Find out what you have.
- If it's been more than a few years, or you don't know, get it pumped.
- Ask the pumper to file the report.

It's a few hundred dollars every few years, and it keeps a $20,000 problem from becoming yours.

## Sidebar: "Septic care in five minutes"

**Pump every 3–5 years.** Ask the hauler to check the baffles and report the pump-out to VDH. Keep the receipt.

**Only the three P's down the toilet: pee, poop, and toilet paper** [9]. "Flushable" wipes aren't, so they go in the trash, along with paper towels, feminine products, grease and cooking oil.

**Spread out the water.** Run laundry over the week, not all on Saturday. Fix running toilets and dripping faucets.

**Protect the drainfield.**
- No parking or driving on it, and no building on it.
- Keep gutters, sump pumps and ditch water pointed away from it.
- Don't plant trees over it.

**Have an alternative system?** It needs a licensed operator's inspection every year, by law [3].

**Call right away if you notice:**
- sewage smells;
- wet or spongy ground over the drainfield;
- slow drains or gurgling pipes;
- a septic alarm going off.

## Editorial notes (not for publication)

**Why this replaces the ecological article.**

- The October 2026 evidence review (`OUTREACH_MESSAGING_REVIEW.md`) found that the earlier article's central claim could not be supported for the Eastern Shore. It linked Shore septic density to creek nitrogen, bacteria and shellfish closures, but no study has measured that link on the Shore. The paper it leaned on (Mitchell et al. 2021) actually found only one overlap between septic hot spots and poor water quality in all of Tidewater Virginia, and does not identify the Shore as a hot spot.
- VDH reports that it is not seeing widespread septic-related shellfish contamination or public-health effects.
- This article leads instead with what is solid and what VDH asked for: the money, the house, the family, and the law.

**The one ecological sentence is deliberate.** "Virginia counts each reported pump-out toward its Chesapeake Bay cleanup goals" explains *why the law exists* without claiming harm we can't document.

**Everything here traces to VDH or the Code.** The single most useful source is VDH's December 2025 report (RD1005), written with the Eastern Shore Health District. Quoting it keeps the article aligned with the agency.

**Confirm with ESHD before publication:**
1. **Pump-out coverage:** the wording "covers most properties; the health district can tell you whether it applies at your address." Accomack's ordinance covers the whole county outside towns; Northampton's covers the whole county; VDH's 2025 report says only Bay-watershed properties must pump out. Ask for one sentence they'd like printed.
2. **Tone of the letter description:** that ESHD is comfortable with the "it wasn't a fine" framing.
3. **The phone number** they want the public to call (757-787-5886 is the Environmental Health line listed on the district's web page).
4. **Whether to name the free-design Hardship Guidelines** in a newspaper (demand could be high).

**Possible lead photo:** a pump truck at a Shore house, or an ESHD staff member at a community booth with the hauler list.

**Spanish-language version:** recommended. The action list translates cleanly.
