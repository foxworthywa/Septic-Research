# Pathway 5 — Iconic and sensitive species and habitats

## Framing and scope

Pathways 1 through 4 have established the biogeochemical and water-quality consequences of failing onsite wastewater treatment systems (OWTS) on Virginia's Eastern Shore (ESVA): subterranean nitrogen transport through the Columbia surficial aquifer (Pathway 1), tidal-creek and salt-marsh enrichment (Pathway 2), Chesapeake mainstem and ESVA seaside lagoon responses (Pathways 3a, 3b), and shellfish-closure and aquaculture impacts (Pathway 4). Pathway 5 asks a different and more public-facing question: does failing septic infrastructure plausibly contribute to the degradation of habitats for the species that ESVA residents, managers, and visitors recognize and care about — the oysters, scallops, blue crabs, terrapins, horseshoe crabs, shorebirds, and feral ponies that define the region's ecological identity?

This pathway is framing-critical. Overclaiming direct septic causation for any of these iconic species would undermine the credibility of the broader synthesis, because in almost every case the proximate drivers of population change are multiple, interacting, and dominated by non-septic stressors: climate warming, sea-level rise, overharvest, disease, predation, incidental take in fishing gear, and historic habitat loss. Understating the links, on the other hand, would miss a real and defensible story: nutrient enrichment of shallow ESVA water bodies degrades habitat quality at the margin for every species on this list, and for several species (notably bay scallops and eelgrass-dependent fauna) the margin of safety is measurably narrow.

The approach taken here is to describe, for each species or species complex, (1) the ecological dependency on a habitat or water-quality condition that septic effluent can alter, (2) the mechanistic pathway from OWTS to that alteration, (3) the ESVA-specific evidence, (4) the analogue evidence from comparable systems where causation has been established, and (5) an explicit evidence-strength label using four tiers: ESTABLISHED (direct causal link documented in the peer-reviewed literature, ESVA or close analogue), STRONGLY SUPPORTED (mechanism well-documented, quantitative data exist but full causal chain is inferential), PLAUSIBLE (mechanism is sound and consistent with the data but direct attribution has not been demonstrated), SPECULATIVE (mechanism is imaginable and consistent with the literature but the weight of evidence does not yet support assertion). A strength-of-evidence table at the end of the section consolidates these labels to make the honesty of the synthesis explicit.

Excluded from this pathway, because they are addressed elsewhere, are: human drinking-water and recreational-water health effects; shellfish aquaculture economics and harvest closures (Pathway 4); and the detailed decline and recovery trajectory of SAV itself, which Pathways 3a and 3b cover at length. Here SAV is treated only as habitat for other species.

## Habitat-forming species

### Submerged aquatic vegetation as habitat for faunal cascades

Pathways 3a and 3b cover SAV decline, recovery, and nutrient-sensitivity in detail; they should be read first for the mechanistic argument. What is specific to Pathway 5 is that SAV beds — primarily eelgrass (*Zostera marina*) on the seaside and in lower Bay salinities, widgeon grass (*Ruppia maritima*) across mid-salinity bayside creeks, and freshwater-to-mesohaline mixed-species meadows at the head of tidal creeks — function as biogenic habitat that structures the lives of several of the iconic species treated below. Orth, Dennison, Lefcheck and colleagues (2017, BioScience 67:698) describe SAV as the mid-Atlantic coast's most important foundation species: the meadow provides three-dimensional structure, refuge from predation, food in the form of epiphytes and blades themselves, wave attenuation, sediment stabilization, and biogeochemical filtration. When a meadow is lost, the fauna it supported are forced into habitats with higher predation risk, lower food availability, and poorer water quality.

Lefcheck et al. (2019, PNAS 116:20636) used four decades of Chesapeake monitoring data to show that SAV extent is a positive predictor of juvenile blue crab abundance, bay anchovy biomass, silver perch, and several other finfish; they quantified the functional loss of habitat services when SAV area declines and the compounding effects of nutrient pollution. Lefcheck, Orth et al. (2018, PNAS 115:3658) established the Bay-wide inverse relationship between watershed nitrogen loading and SAV extent, with a ~23% area recovery attributable to TN load reductions. Aoki, McGlathery et al. (2020, Front Mar Sci) extended this to the ESVA seaside restoration: faunal colonization (bivalves, amphipods, juvenile fish) tracked structural recovery of the seed-planted *Zostera* meadow with a 4- to 6-year lag. Orth, Lefcheck, McGlathery et al. (2020, Sci Adv 6:eabc6434) quantified post-restoration increases in invertebrate density, fish biomass, and water clarity — providing the first rigorous demonstration that restoring seagrass in a nutrient-limited lagoon re-establishes not just the plant but the fauna that depend on it.

The implication for Pathway 5 is that any process that erodes SAV extent or condition — including incremental N loading from failing septic systems — cascades through a broad set of species. This does not mean every faunal decline in Chesapeake or seaside waters is septic-driven; it means the habitat-quality floor on which these species depend is sensitive to N loading at the margin, and the margin on ESVA — particularly in the restored seaside meadow — is quantitatively narrow. Evidence strength: SAV-as-habitat coupling is ESTABLISHED; the marginal contribution of ESVA septic specifically to SAV change is PLAUSIBLE but not quantitatively attributed.

### Eastern oyster as reef-forming ecosystem engineer

Pathway 4 treats the eastern oyster (*Crassostrea virginica*) as an aquaculture species and as a bellwether for bacterial and viral water-quality closures. Here the focus is the ecological role of oyster reefs as ecosystem engineers, and the more subtle ways nutrient enrichment can degrade reef function independently of closure-based management.

Newell (2004, J Shellfish Res 23:51) and Grabowski et al. (2012, BioScience 62:900) established that oyster reefs provide quantifiable ecosystem services beyond harvest: water filtration, denitrification at the reef–sediment interface, shoreline protection, and three-dimensional habitat for mobile fauna. Reef-associated communities include mud crabs, xanthid crabs, naked gobies, oyster toadfish, grass shrimp, and a suite of polychaetes and amphipods; reef-to-fish production links have been quantified for striped bass, red drum, and speckled trout. Oyster reefs in the ESVA — in the Bayside tidal creeks, in protected seaside lagoon margins, and as part of TNC and VIMS restoration projects — are a small fraction of their pre-1880 extent but remain ecologically load-bearing.

The septic-relevant mechanism is indirect but real. Moderate nutrient enrichment accelerates oyster growth (Kemp et al. 2005), but at high loadings it shifts the food web toward pelagic primary production, increases hypoxia risk in reef-adjacent bottom waters, and promotes the boring sponge *Cliona celata* and related reef-degrading epibionts (Carroll et al. 2015). Elevated summer temperatures combined with moderately enriched, organic-matter-rich waters favor *Perkinsus marinus* (Dermo) and *Haplosporidium nelsoni* (MSX), the two parasitic diseases that have devastated Chesapeake oyster populations since the 1950s (Kennedy, Newell & Eble 1996; Ford & Tripp 1996); La Peyre et al. (2010) documented nutrient–temperature–Dermo interactions experimentally. Denitrification on restored oyster reefs is itself a valuable nutrient-cycling service (Kellogg et al. 2013, Mar Ecol Prog Ser 480:1), meaning that degrading reef condition removes a N sink that would otherwise partly buffer septic inputs. Evidence that ESVA septic measurably affects reef condition is not directly established; the mechanism is PLAUSIBLE and the broader oyster-reef-as-habitat story is ESTABLISHED.

## Faunal species

### Bay scallop (*Argopecten irradians*)

No ESVA species is more tightly coupled to the seaside septic-loading story than the bay scallop. Bay scallops are obligate eelgrass associates for most of their juvenile life: newly settled spat attach to *Zostera marina* blades as byssally attached recruits, using the vertical leaf as both substrate and refuge from benthic predators (Peterson and Heck 2001; Carroll et al. 2010). When eelgrass meadows collapse, bay scallop populations collapse on a near-identical timescale — the species is a textbook case of obligate foundation-species dependency (Peterson and Heck 2001; Orth et al. 2006).

Bay scallops were extirpated from Virginia seaside waters by the 1933 hurricane and the subsequent eelgrass wasting disease, and remained absent for roughly 75 years. Following the VIMS-led *Zostera* seed restoration that began in 1999 and the attendant recovery of meadow structure (Orth et al. 2020), scallops were reintroduced to the restored meadows in South Bay beginning in 2009 and expanded through the 2010s. By the 2020s the reintroduced population had reached densities of ~0.1 scallops m⁻² at some sites and was in an exponential phase (Aoki, McGlathery, and Orth 2020; VIMS monitoring, cited in Pathway 3b). The restoration is globally unique — no other bay scallop reintroduction has succeeded at this scale — but the population rests entirely on the continued health of the seaside eelgrass meadow.

The septic-relevant mechanism is inherited from Pathway 3b: any process that erodes the N-loading safety margin of the seaside lagoons — including incremental N inputs from failing septic systems on the seaside uplands — threatens the eelgrass meadow and therefore the scallop. Analogue-region evidence is strong and cautionary. The *Aureococcus anophagefferens* brown tides that began in the Peconic estuary (NY) in 1985 and returned repeatedly through the 1990s and 2000s collapsed both the eelgrass meadow and the bay scallop fishery, and were attributed in part to N loading from dense residential septic development (Gobler, Boneillo, and Debenham 2005; Kinney and Valiela 2011; Gobler and Sunda 2012). Great South Bay (NY) followed a similar trajectory. The Peconic and Great South Bay examples are not ESVA — regulatory regimes and flushing differ — but they demonstrate that residential septic nitrogen in a shallow lagoon can drive a regime shift that ends scallop fisheries.

Evidence strength: scallop dependency on eelgrass is ESTABLISHED; ESVA septic contribution to eelgrass decline is PLAUSIBLE; direct attribution of any ESVA scallop population change to septic loading is SPECULATIVE because the restored population is still in its early exponential phase and has not been tested by a loading-driven stressor.

### Blue crab (*Callinectes sapidus*)

The blue crab is the Chesapeake's iconic wild-capture fishery and one of ESVA's most economically and culturally important species. Juvenile blue crabs use SAV meadows and salt marsh edges as nursery habitat — Orth and van Montfrans (1987) and Lipcius et al. (2005) documented that juvenile survival is an order of magnitude higher in SAV beds than in bare substrate, and Hines (2007) and Seitz et al. (2003) established that SAV acreage and quality are direct predictors of age-0 and age-1 crab abundance. The 2022 Chesapeake Bay Stock Assessment Committee report flagged a historically low total blue crab abundance, with recruitment pressure a likely contributor (CBSAC 2022). The population is now monitored annually through the Winter Dredge Survey; total abundance has fluctuated substantially in the 2020s.

Several septic-relevant mechanisms operate simultaneously. First, SAV loss driven in part by nutrient loading reduces nursery habitat capacity (Lefcheck, Wilcox et al. 2017, 2018; see Pathway 3a). Second, nutrient-driven hypoxia in deeper channel waters displaces and stresses adult crabs (Long, Seitz, and Montagna 2014). Third, oyster reef degradation — indirectly linked to nutrient loading as above — removes another nursery and refuge habitat (Grabowski et al. 2012). Fourth, fecal-indicator contamination from septic effluent can co-occur with HAB events that affect crab physiology, though direct documentation is sparse.

The honest assessment is that blue crab population change on ESVA is driven by a tangle of factors — temperature, predation, harvest regulation, SAV, winter mortality — in which septic-derived nitrogen is one of several inputs, not the dominant driver. Nonetheless, because the species' nursery function is SAV-coupled and ESVA SAV is N-sensitive, the link from septic loading to blue crab habitat quality is mechanistically sound. Evidence: SAV-nursery coupling ESTABLISHED; septic-specific contribution PLAUSIBLE; direct attribution of ESVA blue crab change to septic SPECULATIVE.

### Diamondback terrapin (*Malaclemys terrapin*)

The northern diamondback terrapin is the only true brackish-water turtle in North America and is concentrated in mid-Atlantic tidal creeks and salt marshes — ESVA is one of its core ranges. Terrapins are marsh and creek obligates, feeding primarily on periwinkles (*Littoraria irrorata*), blue crabs, fiddler crabs, ribbed mussels, and small fish (Roosenburg 1991; Bilkovic et al. 2015). Nests are placed on sandy dunes, berms, and occasionally on spoil islands and sandy upland edges just above the high-tide line. The species is listed as a Species of Greatest Conservation Need in Virginia and as Threatened or Endangered in several adjacent states; principal threats include crab-pot drowning (the species is small enough to enter recreational crab pots), road mortality, nest depredation by raccoons, and sea-level-rise-driven nest-site loss (Isdell et al. 2015; Bilkovic et al. 2015).

Septic-relevant mechanisms are indirect but plausible. First, nutrient-driven changes in the tidal-creek benthic community (Pathway 2) can alter the abundance of the small mollusks and crustaceans that make up the terrapin's prey base; whether the net effect is positive or negative is uncertain because some prey taxa (e.g., fiddler crabs) are tolerant or even favored by moderate enrichment, while others are sensitive to hypoxia. Second, bacterial and viral contamination of tidal creeks from failing septic drainfields is a documented water-quality risk for the sympatric human population and for filter-feeding shellfish, but effects on terrapin immunity, parasite load, or embryo development have not been studied. Third, the same small tidal-creek-head parcels most affected by septic failure on ESVA are also primary terrapin nesting and foraging habitats, meaning the spatial overlap between failing septics and the species is high even if the causal chain is not resolved.

Evidence: ESVA concentration of terrapin populations ESTABLISHED (Isdell et al. 2015; Bilkovic et al. 2015); terrapin sensitivity to marsh habitat loss ESTABLISHED; specific septic attribution SPECULATIVE.

### Horseshoe crab (*Limulus polyphemus*) and red knot (*Calidris canutus rufa*)

The Delaware Bay is the global hotspot for spring spawning of the American horseshoe crab, but the ESVA seaside and bayside host significant secondary spawning beaches — Chincoteague, Assateague, Wallops, Cedar Island, Parramore Island, and others (Smith et al. 2017; ASMFC 2019). Horseshoe crab eggs deposited in sandy intertidal sediments during May and June are the critical fuel for the northbound migration of the rufa red knot, a shorebird federally listed as Threatened in 2014 under the U.S. Endangered Species Act. Red knots stage on Delmarva beaches for roughly two to three weeks each spring, doubling their body mass on horseshoe crab eggs before continuing to Arctic breeding grounds (Niles et al. 2008; Baker et al. 2004). The rufa population has declined by more than 75% since the 1980s.

Septic-relevant mechanisms for this pair are the weakest in the entire synthesis, and the evidence is correspondingly thin. Horseshoe crab spawning sediments are intertidal sands that may receive episodic bacterial and nutrient input from adjacent upland septic failures, particularly where shoreline housing density is high. Dungan, Arens, and Watson (2008) and subsequent work have documented horseshoe crab embryo sensitivity to sediment temperature, salinity, and porewater chemistry, but the peer-reviewed literature does not directly address septic-derived bacterial or nutrient exposure as a threat to embryo development at documented field concentrations. For red knots, the stopover habitat requirement is abundant horseshoe crab eggs and a disturbance-free beach; direct septic effects on the shorebirds themselves are not documented.

Evidence: the horseshoe crab / red knot coupling is ESTABLISHED (Niles et al. 2008); septic-specific effects on either species are SPECULATIVE. The species are included here because the potential pathway exists and because the ESVA spawning beaches sit close to residentially developed shorelines, but overclaiming causation would misrepresent the literature.

### Piping plover (*Charadrius melodus*) and barrier-island shorebirds

Piping plover (Atlantic Coast population, federally Threatened), American oystercatcher, Wilson's plover, and least tern nest on ESVA's barrier island beaches and adjacent overwash flats — including Fisherman Island NWR, Cape Charles NWR, and the Assateague/Chincoteague complex to the north. The species are ground nesters, rely on open sandy beach with invertebrate-rich wrackline and intertidal foraging, and are threatened principally by human disturbance, predators (ghost crabs, raccoons, red fox), sea-level rise, and storm-driven nest loss (USFWS 2020; Nol and Humphrey 2020). Freshwater lenses on the barrier islands support small but important invertebrate prey bases on the backbarrier flats.

Septic-relevant mechanisms for barrier-island shorebirds are essentially absent because the islands themselves are unsewered and largely unpopulated; septic impact on barrier island bird habitat is indirect, via changes to receiving-water quality, and the pathway is several steps removed from any direct effect on the birds. Where septic effluent from mainland ESVA enters lagoons and backbarrier bays (Pathway 3b), it can alter the invertebrate communities on intertidal flats where plovers and oystercatchers feed, but this effect has not been measured. Evidence: SPECULATIVE.

### Chincoteague ponies

The Chincoteague pony herd, made famous by the Beebe books and the annual Pony Swim, is a feral horse population grazing the saltmarsh and maritime forest of Assateague Island. The Virginia herd is maintained at roughly 150 animals by the Chincoteague Volunteer Fire Company (National Park Service 2023). Ponies graze primarily on *Spartina alterniflora*, *S. patens*, and associated marsh vegetation and are thought to depend on small freshwater sources on the island.

Septic-relevant mechanisms are indirect and the species is included here primarily because of public recognition rather than weight of evidence. Nutrient enrichment of salt marshes can increase above-ground *Spartina* biomass (Deegan et al. 2012; Pathway 2) and might, in principle, alter forage quality and quantity. There is also an ironic historical anecdote specific to Chincoteague: since the 1970s the town has imported drinking water via pipeline from Wallops Island (NASA) because its own groundwater was contaminated by septic systems during the mid-20th century — demonstrating that septic-driven groundwater impairment has already materially affected the island's water supply (Bay Journal 2005; Chincoteague town history). Whether the feral pony population is itself affected by septic-driven vegetation change is not documented. Evidence: SPECULATIVE.

### Short mentions

**Ribbed mussel (*Geukensia demissa*).** Ribbed mussels are critical N-cycling infauna in Chesapeake salt marshes, forming a mutualistic relationship with *Spartina alterniflora* in which the mussel fertilizes the grass while the grass provides physical support (Bertness 1984; Kreeger and Newell 2001). Mussels filter creek water at rates that are significant at marsh-edge scale, and they sequester fecal-indicator bacteria and pathogens from the water column. Septic-derived nitrogen and pathogen loading thus directly loads an organism that is itself a key regulator of marsh N cycling. Evidence: mechanism ESTABLISHED, ESVA-specific attribution PLAUSIBLE.

**Fiddler crabs (*Minuca pugnax*, *Uca pugilator*).** Fiddler crabs are widely used as indicators of salt marsh health because their burrow networks oxygenate marsh peat and regulate below-ground decomposition. Moderate nutrient enrichment tends to increase fiddler crab density, but severe enrichment and the associated peat collapse (Deegan et al. 2012) eliminate suitable burrow substrate. Evidence: mechanism ESTABLISHED, ESVA attribution PLAUSIBLE.

**Migratory waterfowl.** ESVA's tidal creeks and seaside lagoons are a critical Atlantic Flyway wintering ground for black duck, brant, scaup, canvasback, and numerous dabbling duck species. Brant, in particular, depend on eelgrass and widgeon grass for winter forage, linking waterfowl ecology directly to SAV condition (Perry and Deller 1996). Evidence: SAV-waterfowl coupling ESTABLISHED; septic-specific contribution PLAUSIBLE.

**Bald eagle.** The mid-Atlantic bald eagle recovery is one of the Clean Water Act's success stories, and ESVA is part of that recovery. Eagles are top predators in the Bay food web and are sensitive to bioaccumulative contaminants; septic-derived PPCPs and PFAS (Pathway 1) could in principle accumulate up the food chain, but direct evidence linking ESVA septic sources to eagle contaminant burdens does not exist. Evidence: SPECULATIVE.

The honest summary of this table is that three evidence patterns dominate. (1) For habitat-forming species and the fauna most tightly coupled to SAV or to salt marsh structure, the mechanism is well established and the ESVA-specific septic attribution is PLAUSIBLE — defensibly supported but not quantitatively proven. (2) For species that depend on SAV indirectly (blue crab, bay scallop, waterfowl), the habitat-mediated link is strong but the attribution of ESVA population change to septic specifically is SPECULATIVE given competing drivers. (3) For several iconic species that the public most closely associates with ESVA — the Chincoteague ponies, horseshoe crabs, red knot, piping plover — the septic pathway is real in principle but the direct evidence is absent or very weak, and they are included here for completeness and honest scoping rather than because they are the strongest links.

## Data gaps

Because this pathway has the widest range of evidence strengths of any in the synthesis, the data gaps are specific to individual species or species complexes rather than common to the whole pathway.

1. **Bay scallop population resilience to nutrient loading.** The restored ESVA seaside bay scallop population is the only one of its kind globally, and its sensitivity to incremental N loading has not been tested. A modeling study coupling Hesamfar et al. (in review) groundwater N delivery to *Zostera* meadow condition and to recruitment dynamics would quantify how close the restoration is to its safety margin.

2. **Quantitative SAV-to-faunal-cascade attribution.** Lefcheck et al. (2018, 2019) established that SAV area predicts faunal abundance at Bay-wide scale. A parallel analysis at ESVA-creek scale, partitioned by loading source, has not been done. This could be framed as an ESCC capstone using public CBP monitoring data.

3. **Blue crab nursery habitat sensitivity to local N loading.** The CBSAC 2022 assessment motivates a targeted analysis of how ESVA sub-basin blue crab recruitment tracks local SAV status and N loading, distinct from Bay-wide drivers. VIMS juvenile crab surveys and the CBP CAST model are the starting datasets.

4. **Terrapin prey base and exposure pathways on ESVA.** No study has measured how ESVA terrapin population density, body condition, or parasite burden varies with watershed septic density. Isdell, Chambers, and Bilkovic at VIMS are the natural collaborators.

5. **Horseshoe crab embryo exposure to septic-derived contaminants on ESVA spawning beaches.** The peer-reviewed literature has not addressed whether adjacent-upland septic contamination affects horseshoe crab embryo development at documented field concentrations. This is a genuinely open question and a publication-worthy study design.

6. **Ribbed mussel filtration capacity as a creek-scale septic mitigation metric.** If ribbed mussels filter septic-derived bacteria and sequester N at creek-scale rates that matter, they represent both a natural attenuation service and an exposure pathway. The data exist in the general ecology literature but have not been applied to ESVA creeks.

7. **Barrier-island freshwater-lens ecology.** The backbarrier freshwater lenses that support invertebrate prey bases for nesting shorebirds on Fisherman, Parramore, and Cedar Islands are under-studied, and their sensitivity to incremental nutrient loading from septic sources on the mainland via tidal exchange has not been characterized.

8. **Honest baseline for the Chincoteague ponies / horseshoe crab / red knot pathway.** These three species are the ones most likely to appear in public-facing outreach about ESVA ecology. Before any outreach materials are produced, a scoping study that lays out the actual evidence for each is essential — the risk of overclaiming is highest here.
