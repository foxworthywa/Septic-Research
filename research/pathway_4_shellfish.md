# Pathway 4 — Shellfish grounds, aquaculture, and commercial fisheries

## Mechanism of harm

Failing onsite wastewater treatment systems (OWTS) affect shellfish grounds, aquaculture operations, and commercial fisheries on the Eastern Shore of Virginia (ESVA) through two coupled mechanisms: (1) delivery of fecal indicator bacteria and human enteric viruses that trigger regulatory harvest closures, and (2) delivery of nutrients and labile organic carbon that alter water quality and habitat in ways that affect shellfish physiology, recruitment, and the nursery habitat that supports wild-capture fisheries. The two mechanisms operate on different timescales — hours to days for pathogen pulses, months to decades for water-quality and habitat endpoints — but reach the same receiving waters through the hydrologic pathways described in Pathways 1, 2, 3a, and 3b.

*Fecal-indicator and pathogen delivery.* A drainfield that is hydraulically overloaded, inundated by a high water table, or too close to a tidal creek discharges partially treated effluent carrying ~10^6–10^8 fecal coliform MPN 100 mL⁻¹ into the shallow subsurface. In sandy coastal-plain aquifers with short flow paths, a substantial fraction of that bacterial load reaches tidal receiving water within hours to a few days of a rain event — faster than the seawater die-off T90 of ~1–3 days. *Crassostrea virginica* (adult filtration ~5 L h⁻¹) and *Mercenaria mercenaria* concentrate particle-associated bacteria in their digestive tissue by orders of magnitude above ambient seawater. The National Shellfish Sanitation Program (NSSP), implemented in Virginia by the VDH Division of Shellfish Safety (DSS), classifies growing waters as Approved (30-sample geometric mean ≤14 fecal coliform MPN 100 mL⁻¹, ≤10% exceeding 43 MPN 5-tube), Conditionally Approved (closed for a specified period after a rainfall trigger, typically ≥1 inch in 24 h for three days in Virginia), Restricted, Conditionally Restricted, and Prohibited. The thresholds were originally set against typhoid and hepatitis A and are routinely tripped by OWTS, stormwater, wildlife, and livestock sources.

Fecal coliform is a poor proxy for human enteric viruses. Norovirus GI/GII dominates shellfish-associated illness, persists in seawater and shellfish tissue for weeks to months (an order of magnitude longer than fecal coliform), and is transferred from specifically human sources (sewage, septic, CSOs, boat discharge) rather than wildlife or livestock. Lipp, Farrah & Rose (2001), Campos et al. (2015, 2017), and Lowther et al. (2021, Frontiers in Microbiology) established the discordance: shellfish samples meeting fecal coliform criteria routinely carry detectable norovirus RNA, and standard 48–72 h depuration clears *E. coli* but fails to clear norovirus from oyster digestive diverticula. The implication is that OWTS failures can deliver virologically relevant doses of human pathogens to shellfish grounds even when fecal-coliform monitoring finds waters compliant — a false-negative risk that biases conventional closure systems toward the older pathogen suite they were designed against.

*Nutrient- and water-quality-mediated physiological effects.* The same OWTS discharges carry DIN/DON, phosphorus, labile DOC, surfactants, and trace pharmaceuticals. Nitrogen loading to shallow estuarine and lagoonal systems drives increased phytoplankton and picoplankton biomass, HABs, larger diel pH and DO swings, seasonal bottom hypoxia, and reduced aragonite saturation from respiration-driven CO2 accumulation (coastal acidification). Each endpoint has a documented physiological signature in *C. virginica* and *M. mercenaria*: elevated larval mortality and reduced shell deposition under low Ωaragonite (Waldbusser et al. 2015; Gobler & Talmage 2013); reduced clearance rates and energetic stress under chronic hypoxia; reduced growth during *Aureococcus*, *Aureoumbra*, and *Alexandrium* blooms; and recruitment failure when seagrass nursery habitat (a structural requirement for bay scallop *Argopecten irradians*) collapses. Effects grade from sub-lethal energy-budget costs at moderate enrichment to population-level failure at the tipping points described for shallow lagoons in Pathway 3b.

*Habitat mediation and economic-ecological coupling.* OWTS-delivered nutrients affect the structural habitat commercial fisheries depend on: eelgrass (*Zostera marina*) as obligate bay-scallop substrate and juvenile blue-crab (*Callinectes sapidus*) nursery, and oyster reefs as biogenic habitat for juvenile finfish and invertebrates (Newell 2004; Coen et al. 2007). Pathway 4 inherits the nutrient–SAV and nutrient–reef couplings from Pathways 3a/3b and focuses on the fisheries consequences. For an aquaculture lease or wild-harvest bar, these ecological impacts translate into economic loss via closure days × gear types × landed value: a lease in a Conditionally Approved zone loses product access for a fixed period per rain trigger, and if triggers cross often enough the area becomes Restricted or Prohibited and the lease loses commercial utility entirely. The distinctive feature of Pathway 4 is that its endpoint is a regulated economic activity with continuous monitoring — so even when source attribution is imperfect, consequences are visible in the regulatory record.

## ESVA-specific evidence

*Industry scale.* The Eastern Shore is the center of gravity of Virginia's shellfish aquaculture industry. The VIMS 2024 Situation & Outlook Report (Hudson, released November 2025) put Virginia shellfish aquaculture direct farm-gate sales at $81 million in 2024 — a 52% increase over the $53 million of 2018, the highest total on record — with over 700 jobs (up from 480 in 2018), the great majority of both in Accomack and Northampton. Virginia leads the nation in cultivated hard clam production (~99% ESVA) and its cultivated oyster sector surpassed pre-pandemic levels in 2024. VMRC records add wild hard-clam, public-ground oyster, and ESVA blue-crab landings; blue crab uses ESVA creeks as nursery habitat.

*Classified growing waters and the DSS shoreline-survey program.* VDH DSS classifies roughly 20–25 named growing areas along the ESVA Bayside and seaside (e.g., Area 075 Pocomoke Sound, Area 084 Occohannock Creek, Area 086 Nassawadox Creek; seaside areas covering Folly Creek, Machipongo River, Hog Island Bay, South Bay, Magothy Bay). Each area is monitored at 6–30 adverse-pollution-condition stations, with a minimum of six samples per station per year, and re-surveyed on a 12-year intensive-shoreline cycle. Under the NSSP Model Ordinance (FDA 2019), DSS staff walk or boat the shoreline, enumerate every residence, confirm sewer vs. OWTS connection, inspect drainfields for surface discharge, and map straight pipes, failing lift stations, marina pump-outs, and livestock access. Each growing-area survey (publicly released as a numbered PDF) therefore functions as a parcel-level OWTS inventory in the shellfish-relevant buffer.

*Condemnation areas.* DSS shoreline-survey documents for Accomack and Northampton show that essentially every ESVA tidal creek with adjacent residential development carries at least one condemnation area (Restricted or Prohibited) surrounding suspected fecal sources. Named Bayside examples include Pungoteague, Nandua, Onancock, and Occohannock Creeks, Pocomoke Sound near Saxis and Sanford, Hungars Creek, Nassawadox Creek, and Cherrystone Inlet; seaside examples include Chincoteague Bay near the town of Chincoteague, Assawoman and Gargathy Bays, Folly Creek above Wachapreague, Machipongo River above Willis Wharf, and creeks around Oyster and Cape Charles. The spatial pattern is consistent: condemnation zones cluster in the upper reaches of creeks — low-flushing, residentially developed, OWTS-dense headwaters, the highest-vulnerability geometry identified in Pathway 2. Growing-area reports suggest the proportion of ESVA classified waters in Conditionally Approved or more-restrictive classes runs on the order of 15–30%, a share that has crept upward over two decades with shoreline development and SLR-driven OWTS inundation.

*Rain-triggered Conditionally Approved closures.* DSS maintains an active Conditional Shellfish Harvesting Area Status page updated after every event. ESVA triggers are typically ≥1 inch rainfall in 24 h with three-day closure; some creeks carry more sensitive ≥0.5-inch triggers. Closure-day counts can exceed 30–60 days per year in wet years for headwater-creek segments. Zimmer-Faust et al. (2018, *Water Research*), using 11 years of Chesapeake shellfish-water data, found the percent of samples exceeding FDA fecal coliform criteria rose from 7% at <1 inch rainfall to 37% at ≥1 inch in 24 h — a quantitative validation of the trigger. Source attribution in DSS reports is qualitative; for small residentially developed ESVA headwater creeks with minor agricultural input and diffuse wildlife roosts, OWTS is usually the leading suspected source.

*OWTS exposure and sea-level rise.* Mitchell et al. (2021, *Frontiers in Marine Science* 8:631757) mapped parcel-level OWTS vulnerability across coastal Virginia and identified the Eastern Shore as the most exposed locality cluster, with failure hot spots fairly evenly distributed along the length of the Shore and concentrated in the shoreline-proximal, low-elevation parcels that sit directly over the classified shellfish waters. The Virginia Wastewater Infrastructure Policy Working Group (RD883, 2021) named Oyster, Willis Wharf, Quinby, Wachapreague, Saxis, Chincoteague, and Cape Charles as particularly exposed. Capps et al. (2020, *Environmental Science & Technology*) added the socio-economic framing — unsewered ESVA parcels are disproportionately low-income and costly to sewer. Together these three lines establish near-complete spatial overlap between vulnerable septic and classified shellfish waters on ESVA.

*ESVA-specific response studies.* The VIMS Eastern Shore Laboratory (Wachapreague) and VIMS Marine Advisory Program have documented industry concerns about rising closure-day counts and warm-season HAB events in Bayside creeks and seaside lagoons. ABC-selected oyster lines used by ESVA growers are bred for disease and growth, not for pathogen- or nutrient-stress tolerance, so water-quality shifts pass directly to production. The ESVA bay scallop reintroduction is a clean local illustration of habitat mediation: scallops reappeared only after seagrass returned, and population trajectory is tightly coupled to eelgrass extent, which is nutrient-sensitive (Pathway 3b). No published ESVA study has microbial-source-tracked a fraction of closure days or production loss to OWTS — the leading local data gap for Pathway 4.

## Analogue evidence

The analogue record converges from several independent directions on the same mechanistic story: OWTS-dense coastal watersheds produce quantifiable increases in shellfish fecal-indicator and virus contamination, closure days, and longer-term nutrient- and habitat-mediated effects on shellfish populations.

*Buzzards Bay and Waquoit, Cape Cod — the septic-closure case study.* The Buzzards Bay National Estuary Program maintains the most systematic published record linking residential OWTS to shellfish closures on the US East Coast: approximately 24% of Buzzards Bay growing waters are classified in Conditionally Approved or more-restrictive classes, and upward trends in closed acreage over 1985–2020 are statistically linked to shoreline housing density (a direct OWTS proxy) in Wareham, Marion, Mattapoisett, Fairhaven, and Dartmouth sub-watersheds. The Waquoit Bay NERR provides the complementary nitrogen-loading attribution record: Valiela et al. (1997, *Ecological Applications*) developed the Nitrogen Loading Model and found residential OWTS contributing 40–80% of delivered N to Waquoit sub-estuaries with high residential density. Waquoit *Mya arenaria* and quahog grounds were progressively closed through the 1980s–1990s as OWTS-driven degradation advanced. Buzzards Bay and Waquoit remain the canonical septic-to-shellfish-closure analogues for East Coast sandy coastal-plain settings and are directly mappable onto ESVA.

*Narragansett Bay, Rhode Island — the sewer-extension quasi-experiment.* RIDEM's Conditionally Approved Shellfish Area program is analogous to Virginia's. Greenwich Bay, Potowomut, Wickford, and the Narrow River watershed were historically the most closure-exposed areas; sewer extension into portions of these watersheds between 2000 and 2020 produced measurable reopening of previously closed shellfish acreage — a quasi-experimental demonstration that removing OWTS from a shellfish watershed restores harvestable water. RI Shellfish Sector Working Group reports quantified the reopening at roughly 500 acres, principally attributable to the Warwick sewer extension.

*Pamlico Sound and NC ShellCast.* The NC Division of Marine Fisheries has documented sustained increases in Conditionally Approved acreage driven by OWTS and stormwater sources, with hurricanes and extreme-rainfall events producing regional-scale closures lasting weeks to months. The NC State ShellCast forecast tool (Nelson et al. 2022) provides operational rainfall-based closure prediction and produces watershed-specific dose-response curves between rainfall totals and fecal indicator exceedance; its underlying datasets confirm that OWTS-dense coastal-plain settings have the steepest rainfall-closure response curves.

*Indian River Lagoon — nutrient-driven shellfish and seagrass cascade.* The Florida IRL is the nearest US case in which residential OWTS has been directly implicated in a lagoon-scale shellfish and seagrass collapse. Lapointe et al. (2015, 2017, 2020, 2023) used δ¹⁵N fingerprinting to attribute the bulk of bioavailable N loading to OWTS; the 2023 paper reported ~79% septic, ~21% fertilizer. The 2011-onset brown-tide, cyanobacterial, and *Caulerpa prolifera* cascade (Phlips et al. 2015) removed >60% of IRL seagrass, crashed bay scallop and hard-clam populations, and triggered manatee mass mortality. The IRL is more urbanized than ESVA but lagoon physics, sandy coastal-plain aquifer, and OWTS-density pattern are broadly comparable. The IRL is the strongest published demonstration that OWTS nutrients can drive a shallow coastal lagoon past a shellfish-relevant tipping point within a decade.

*Great South Bay — the brown-tide–bay-scallop collapse.* Long Island's Great South Bay supported the most productive *A. irradians* fishery in the US before 1985. The initial *Aureococcus anophagefferens* bloom that year crashed the scallop population and the fishery has never recovered. Gobler et al. (2005, 2017) documented that brown tide thrives on DON; Kinney & Valiela (2011) attributed much of Long Island DON loading to residential OWTS. This is an ESVA-relevant cautionary tale because the seaside bay-scallop reintroduction is rebuilding a population in a lagoon within the physical envelope where brown tide has previously collapsed a scallop fishery, and because the OWTS-to-DON linkage is quantitatively established.

*Apalachicola Bay, Florida — the multi-stressor collapse precedent.* The 2012 collapse of the Apalachicola Bay *C. virginica* fishery (Camp et al. 2015) was driven primarily by upstream freshwater withdrawal and post-spill overharvest, not septic. It remains a reference point for ESVA because it demonstrates that a productive East Coast oyster fishery can collapse in months once multiple stressors align, and because the post-2012 record (commercial harvest suspended in 2020 through at least 2025) shows that recovery after crossing a tipping point is neither fast nor guaranteed.

*Norovirus outbreak analogues.* Webby et al. (2007), Iwamoto et al. (2010), and Campos et al. (2017) collectively establish that shellfish-associated norovirus outbreaks routinely trace back to specific human fecal sources in the harvest watershed (OWTS failures, straight pipes, CSOs), that norovirus persists in oyster digestive tissue for weeks at typical winter temperatures, and that outbreaks occur even in waters meeting fecal coliform criteria. The ESVA implication is that fecal-coliform-only closure systems cannot guarantee absence of virologically relevant pathogen exposure in OWTS-affected growing areas.

*Taken together,* the analogue record establishes that (1) OWTS-dense coastal watersheds produce quantifiable increases in shellfish closure days (Buzzards Bay, Narragansett, Pamlico, ShellCast); (2) removing OWTS via sewer extension restores harvestable shellfish acreage (Narragansett Bay); (3) OWTS nutrients can drive coastal-plain lagoons past habitat-mediated shellfish tipping points within a decade (IRL, Great South Bay); and (4) fecal-coliform regulation is systematically insensitive to norovirus risk. All four strands apply to ESVA.

## Strength-of-evidence assessment

Evidence for Pathway 4 is asymmetric: very strong for the pathogen/closure mechanism, strong for the ESVA spatial overlap of OWTS exposure with classified shellfish waters, moderate for the nutrient/physiology mechanism, and weak for direct quantitative attribution of specific ESVA closure days or production losses to OWTS.

*Pathogen/closure mechanism — high confidence.* The linkage from OWTS discharge to fecal coliform elevation in shellfish waters is mechanistically clear and supported by decades of regulatory monitoring. The NSSP Model Ordinance, FDA/ISSC sanitary-survey methodology, and VDH DSS implementation comprise a mature, harmonized framework in which OWTS-dense watersheds are routinely identified as closure contributors. Buzzards Bay, Narragansett, Pamlico, and ShellCast each independently confirm that shellfish-water exceedance probability rises with OWTS density and rainfall-pulse intensity. DSS shoreline surveys on ESVA walk the buffer and enumerate OWTS; the mapping of closed waters onto residentially developed creek headwaters is qualitatively exact.

*ESVA OWTS exposure — high confidence.* Mitchell et al. (2021) and RD883 (2021) independently identify ESVA as the most OWTS-exposed coastal Virginia locality, with parcel-level vulnerability overlapping classified shellfish waters. Capps et al. (2020) confirms the socio-economic geometry. Thousands of ESVA parcels operate OWTS within the hydrogeologic buffer of classified shellfish waters; a non-trivial fraction are already marginal or inundated; and both counts will increase with SLR.

*Virus–indicator discordance — high confidence, ESVA local data low.* Lipp et al. (2001), Campos et al. (2015, 2017), Iwamoto et al. (2010), and Lowther et al. (2021) together close the mechanistic case that fecal coliform is a poor proxy for norovirus and that standard depuration does not clear it. The gap is that no published norovirus survey of ESVA shellfish tissue or water exists; the conclusion for ESVA is imported from the analogue literature.

*Nutrient/physiology mechanism — moderate confidence.* Waldbusser et al. (2015), Gobler & Talmage (2013), and the coastal-acidification literature establish physiological dose-response curves for *C. virginica* and *M. mercenaria* larvae to pH/Ωaragonite; the brown-tide literature establishes HAB impacts on bivalve feeding. The direct link from OWTS N to ESVA shellfish-ground water chemistry rests on importing parameterized curves from other systems — VCR LTER and CBP monitoring are not designed for growing-area-scale attribution.

*Habitat mediation — moderate confidence.* Seagrass loss → bay scallop and juvenile finfish decline is well-established in the Long Island and ESVA-seaside records. Oyster-reef condition → juvenile blue crab and fish nursery function is established in principle (Newell 2004; Coen et al. 2007) but ESVA-specific quantification is limited. Pathways 3a/3b provide the OWTS-to-SAV chain; Pathway 4 inherits it.

*Direct ESVA attribution — low confidence.* The biggest evidentiary weakness. No published ESVA study has quantified what fraction of DSS closure days are attributable to OWTS vs. stormwater, wildlife, or agriculture. DSS shoreline surveys identify candidate sources qualitatively but do not apportion. No ESVA study has used microbial source tracking (Bacteroidales HF183, F+ coliphage, crAssphage, or enteric-virus PCR) to quantify the human-fecal fraction of observed contamination; none has matched OWTS-density gradients to closure-day gradients in a quasi-experimental design.

*Overall.* The case that failing OWTS damage ESVA shellfish grounds rests on a strong analogue record, a strong mechanistic foundation, and a strong spatial-overlap argument, weakened by the absence of direct local attribution. In regulatory and precautionary terms the evidence is more than sufficient to treat OWTS as a first-order threat to the ESVA shellfish aquaculture industry; for specific management decisions (e.g., sewer-extension cost-benefit in a specific creek) it is currently insufficient and requires the targeted studies listed below.

## Data gaps

1. *Microbial source tracking in ESVA shellfish watersheds.* No published Bacteroidales HF183, F+ coliphage, or crAssphage survey pairs ESVA creeks with simultaneous OWTS-density mapping. A targeted MST campaign across an ESVA OWTS-density gradient would directly quantify the OWTS fraction of fecal indicator loading — the attribution DSS shoreline surveys cannot produce.

2. *Quantitative closure-day attribution.* DSS Conditional Shellfish Harvesting Area Status records contain years of closure-day data for every ESVA conditionally approved growing area, but no published analysis has decomposed closure-day counts by source or related closure-day trends to shoreline OWTS density. A retrospective using existing DSS and parcel data would yield a quantitative "closure days per OWTS-density unit" figure for ESVA.

3. *Norovirus prevalence in ESVA shellfish tissue and water.* No published survey of norovirus GI/GII or human enteric adenovirus in ESVA harvest water or shellfish tissue exists. Given the indicator-virus discordance and the industry's reliance on fecal-coliform certification, this is simultaneously a public-health and an ecological gap.

4. *Parcel-level OWTS N loading to specific growing areas.* The Valiela Nitrogen Loading Model has not been parameterized and applied at the ESVA-growing-area scale. Applying it with ESVA-specific attenuation coefficients would produce a parcel-level map of expected N loading to each classified growing area.

5. *Farm-level water-quality records.* Leaseholder-maintained pH, DO, chl-a, and turbidity records exist informally for several ESVA farms but are not collated into a research-quality time series. A formal network — cooperating with the VIMS Eastern Shore Lab — would close the gap between regulatory (fecal coliform only) monitoring and the nutrient/physiology endpoints that drive production.

6. *Economic-ecological coupling.* The VIMS Situation & Outlook Report quantifies aggregate value but does not decompose production losses or revenue volatility by closure days or water-quality events at lease scale. Lease-level production-and-closure datasets would permit "dollars per OWTS-delivered kg N" and "dollars per closure day per unit area" estimates.

7. *SLR trajectory for closure-day burden.* No study has combined Mitchell et al.'s OWTS-inundation projections with rainfall-pulse intensity scenarios and current DSS records to forecast 2030–2050 closure-day burden by ESVA growing area.

8. *Gear-specific exposure geometry.* Cage-cultured floating oysters, bottom-planted oysters, and bottom hard clams experience different exposure profiles to bottom-derived pathogens and HABs; these have not been quantified for ESVA practice.

9. *Disease–water quality interactions.* The interactions between chronic nutrient enrichment and susceptibility to *Perkinsus marinus* (Dermo) and *Haplosporidium nelsoni* (MSX) in ESVA cultured oyster stocks are documented only generally, despite disease being a major production driver.
