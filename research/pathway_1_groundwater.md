# Pathway 1 — Groundwater and the sole-source Columbia aquifer

## Mechanism of harm

Conventional onsite wastewater treatment systems (OWTS) rely on an unsaturated soil treatment area (STA) beneath the drainfield to nitrify ammonium, adsorb phosphorus, attenuate pathogens, and partially degrade organic wastewater contaminants before effluent reaches the water table. Where that unsaturated zone is thin, coarse-textured, or seasonally saturated — conditions characteristic of the Columbia (surficial) aquifer underlying almost all of Virginia's Eastern Shore (ESVA) — the STA is short-circuited and partially treated effluent enters groundwater with a chemical signature that persists for years to decades (Speiran 1996; Richardson 1994).

Nitrogen is the most mobile and most ecologically consequential constituent. Septic effluent typically enters the STA as ~40–80 mg L⁻¹ total N, dominated by ammonium and organic N. Once nitrified to nitrate (NO₃⁻) in the unsaturated zone, nitrate is essentially conservative in the oxic, sandy Columbia aquifer: denitrification rates are low because labile organic carbon is quickly consumed near the infiltrative surface and deeper aquifer sediments are carbon-poor (Speiran 1996; Böhlke 2002). Substantial denitrification on ESVA is restricted to narrow riparian and tidal-fringe zones where groundwater intersects carbon-rich, reducing sediments before discharging to surface water (Speiran 2010; USGS 1997). The result is a well-documented "nitrate conveyor belt" from drainfields through the shallow aquifer to tidal creeks, the bayside lagoonal system, and seaside marshes (Valiela et al. 1997; Mitchell et al. 2021).

Phosphorus is traditionally assumed to be retained by sorption to Fe/Al oxides in the STA, but this retention is finite. Long-operating drainfields in sandy, low-oxide soils — again typical of ESVA's Bojac/Munden soil associations — reach P saturation and exhibit breakthrough, delivering dissolved reactive phosphorus to groundwater and eventually to surface waters (Robertson et al. 1998; Lusk et al. 2017). Acidic, low-Fe coastal plain sands have shorter sorption lifetimes than inland soils, so P breakthrough on ESVA is plausible even for middle-aged systems.

Pharmaceuticals and personal-care products (PPCPs), artificial sweeteners (sucralose, acesulfame), and per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS) pass through conventional STAs with limited attenuation. Cape Cod plume studies have tracked sulfamethoxazole, carbamazepine, sucralose, and short-chain PFAS more than a kilometer downgradient of septic sources (Barber et al. 2015; Schaider et al. 2014; Swartz et al. 2006). Because these compounds are non-volatile and poorly sorbed in sandy aquifers, the Columbia aquifer almost certainly carries a similar chemical cocktail, though direct ESVA measurements are absent.

Subsurface microbial communities are not passive conduits. Septic plumes restructure aquifer microbiomes, enriching ammonia oxidizers, denitrifiers (where electron donors allow), and antibiotic-resistance gene reservoirs, and suppressing oligotrophic native taxa (Amador & Loomis 2020; Carrey et al. 2021). These shifts alter in-situ biogeochemical cycling of N, Fe, Mn, and S, changing the form in which nutrients and metals are delivered to downstream ecosystems.

Two climate-driven processes compound all of the above on ESVA. First, sea-level rise raises the water table across the peninsula, compressing the unsaturated zone beneath drainfields and mechanically short-circuiting treatment; when the water table rises into trench gravel, effluent bypasses the STA entirely (Cox et al. 2019; Mitchell et al. 2021; O'Driscoll et al. 2024). Second, saltwater intrusion along the bayside and seaside margins shifts redox and ionic-strength conditions in the shallow aquifer, potentially remobilizing sorbed phosphorus and ammonium and altering denitrification efficiency (Ardón et al. 2013; Tully et al. 2019). These two processes together mean that ESVA drainfields installed under 1980s–1990s hydrologic conditions are being progressively "de-rated" as treatment infrastructure without any physical change to the system itself.

## ESVA-specific evidence

ESVA is underlain by the Columbia aquifer, a shallow, unconfined, sandy surficial aquifer designated a sole-source aquifer by EPA in 1997 under §1424(e) of the Safe Drinking Water Act (EPA 1997). The designation recognized that essentially all freshwater on the two-county peninsula (Accomack and Northampton) originates as recharge to this aquifer, which in turn discharges to the bayside creeks of Chesapeake Bay and the seaside lagoons and marshes fronting the Virginia Coast Reserve.

The foundational hydrogeologic synthesis is Richardson (1994, USGS WSP 2401), which mapped the Columbia and underlying confined aquifers and showed that groundwater flow paths on ESVA are short (typically <2 km), shallow, and dominated by discharge to tidal shorelines. Speiran's USGS Water-Supply Paper 2479 (1996) then directly addressed nitrate fate along those flow paths, documenting that nitrate concentrations in shallow groundwater beneath agricultural and septic-served areas commonly exceeded 5–10 mg L⁻¹ NO₃-N and that denitrification was limited to thin riparian zones where groundwater encountered reduced, carbon-rich sediments near discharge points. Speiran's follow-on USGS Fact Sheet (1997) and subsequent work (Speiran 2010) emphasized that forested and wetland buffers are necessary but not sufficient controls on N delivery to coastal waters, because much of ESVA's groundwater discharges below or lateral to effective denitrifying zones.

Mitchell, Isdell, Herman, and Tombleson (2021, Frontiers in Marine Science 8:631757) is the most recent ESVA-relevant ecological assessment. Using sea-level-rise scenarios and the best-available state septic database, they showed that rising water tables across Tidewater Virginia — including ESVA — will progressively reduce the unsaturated thickness beneath drainfields, produce "hot spots" of septic failure, and that the data needed to quantify ecological consequences are, as of publication, inadequate at a statewide scale. Hesamfar et al. (in review, J. Hydrology) extends this with a SEAWAT variable-density flow and transport model for portions of ESVA that couples recharge, pumping, and sea-level forcing to project where and when the Columbia aquifer's saline interface will migrate landward and where the water table will intersect existing drainfields. Preliminary results indicate that by mid-century a substantial fraction of bayside and seaside drainfields will have <60 cm vertical separation distance — the threshold below which nitrification collapses (O'Driscoll et al. 2014, 2024).

ESVA-specific ecological (non-human-health) evidence tying septic-derived groundwater chemistry directly to downstream ecological response remains thin. There are no published ESVA studies quantifying PPCPs, PFAS, or antibiotic-resistance genes in the Columbia aquifer beneath septic clusters; no direct ESVA measurements of P breakthrough from aged drainfields; and no published microbial-community surveys of septic plumes in the Columbia aquifer. The Mitchell et al. (2021) and Hesamfar et al. (in review) efforts are essentially exposure models, not effects studies.

## Analogue-region evidence

**Cape Cod, Massachusetts (USGS Toxics plume; Massachusetts Military Reservation).** The most detailed septic/wastewater groundwater plume studies in North America come from Cape Cod, where USGS has tracked a wastewater-derived plume in a sandy, unconfined aquifer for more than three decades (LeBlanc 1984; Barber et al. 2015). Key transferable findings: nitrate, boron, chloride, sucralose, sulfamethoxazole, carbamazepine, and short-chain PFAS move with groundwater over kilometer distances with minimal attenuation; denitrification is patchy and depends on localized organic carbon; and the plume footprint is controlled more by regional groundwater flow than by local attenuation. Cape Cod's outwash sands are a reasonable hydrogeologic analogue to ESVA's Columbia aquifer (both coarse-textured, oxic, low organic carbon), so the contaminant-transport lessons transfer strongly. What does not transfer is Cape Cod's regional flow geometry — flow paths there are far longer than on the narrow ESVA peninsula, so ESVA plumes should reach tidal discharge zones more quickly and with less dilution.

**Rhode Island (URI — Amador, Loomis, Cox).** The University of Rhode Island group has produced the clearest process-level evidence on advanced vs. conventional OWTS performance and on climate-driven failure. Cox, Loomis, and Amador (2019, J. Sustainable Water in the Built Environment 5:04019007) provided preliminary evidence that rising coastal groundwater tables already threaten septic function in Charlestown, RI, documenting systems with <30 cm separation to water table and seasonal saturation of drainfields. Amador and Loomis (2020) synthesize denitrification constraints and microbial community structure beneath drainfields. These findings transfer strongly to ESVA because both settings are low-relief coastal plain with shallow water tables and sandy soils; the RI work essentially provides the mechanistic template for what Mitchell et al. (2021) and Hesamfar et al. (in review) predict for ESVA. What does not transfer cleanly is RI's regulatory push toward advanced N-removal systems, which has no analogue in Virginia's Eastern Shore regulatory regime.

**Dare County, North Carolina (O'Driscoll, Humphrey).** O'Driscoll et al. (2024, J. Water and Climate Change 15:3666) analyzed 1984–2022 groundwater-level records in Dare County and showed mean water-table rise of ~7.6 mm yr⁻¹, tracking relative sea-level rise. They link this rise directly to declining vertical separation distance beneath drainfields and therefore declining nitrification efficiency, building on earlier Humphrey et al. (2015) work showing median groundwater-transported TDN loads ~3× higher from septic watersheds than sewered watersheds in coastal NC. This is the closest published analogue to ESVA in terms of geomorphology, climate, and septic density: Outer Banks barrier and backbarrier settings are hydrologically and ecologically similar to seaside ESVA. Transfer is strong; the main caveat is that Dare County's barrier-island hydrology is more extreme than most of ESVA, so Dare findings likely bracket the upper end of ESVA impacts.

**Coastal Georgia (Hoghooghi, Pippin, Bledsoe).** Hoghooghi et al. (2021, PLoS ONE 16:e0256606) built a GIS framework combining a modified Soil Topographic Index with seasonal high water-table data to map septic vulnerability across coastal Georgia, and showed that both hydrologic setting and system specifications predict repair/replacement events. The framework is directly portable to ESVA and offers a template for regional vulnerability mapping. What transfers: the methodology and the finding that shallow seasonal water tables dominate failure risk. What does not: coastal Georgia's thicker saprolitic soils differ from ESVA's sandy Columbia aquifer, so absolute failure rates are not directly comparable.
