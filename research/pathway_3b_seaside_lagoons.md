# Pathway 3b — The ESVA Seaside coastal lagoon system

## Mechanism of harm

The ESVA seaside coastal lagoon system comprises a chain of shallow (mean depth ~1–2 m), microtidal-to-mesotidal bays impounded behind the Virginia barrier islands: South Bay, Cobb Bay, Hog Island Bay, Spider Crab Bay, Ramshorn Bay, Mockhorn Bay, Magothy Bay, and the Machipongo system. Unlike the Bayside mainstem (Pathway 3a), these lagoons receive essentially no surface river input. There are no watershed-scale rivers on the seaside; the peninsula's low-relief, sandy surficial Columbia aquifer drains directly into the lagoons as submarine and intertidal groundwater discharge (SGD) and via short, first-order tidal creeks. This makes the seaside a textbook case of a groundwater-dominated coastal lagoon, in which the watershed nitrogen signal propagates to receiving waters almost exclusively through the shallow aquifer.

The mechanism by which failing septic systems could damage this system runs in four steps. First, onsite wastewater N (largely as NH4+ near the drainfield, oxidized to NO3– within a few meters of the unsaturated zone) enters the Columbia aquifer. The surficial Columbia aquifer has transit times of years to a few decades at the scale of the peninsula, but much shorter (months to years) for houses sited within a few hundred meters of the lagoon shore. Second, groundwater emerges into lagoon sediments and intertidal flats as SGD. Because the lagoons are shallow and poorly flushed, even modest N fluxes produce disproportionately large increases in water-column dissolved inorganic nitrogen (DIN) and dissolved organic nitrogen (DON) relative to deeper, better-flushed systems. Third, shallow, clear-water lagoons of this kind are known to respond to N enrichment in a strongly nonlinear fashion. The classic Cloern (2001) and Duarte (2009) conceptual models describe a cascade in which modest N increases first favor macroalgal canopies (Ulva, Gracilaria vermiculophylla) over rooted seagrass, then tip toward picoplankton/brown-tide dominance as macroalgae themselves shade out. These regime shifts can be crossed at DIN thresholds far below the values that matter in the Chesapeake mainstem — on the order of 5–15 μM DIN, or <30 g N m-2 yr-1 areal loading, depending on residence time. Fourth, once seagrass is lost, a feedback loop (resuspension, bioturbation, loss of oxygen pumping into sediments, loss of N burial in below-ground biomass) locks the system in a turbid, macroalgal or phytoplankton-dominated state that is difficult to reverse.

The seaside system is therefore vulnerable to septic N through a distinctively short and direct hydrologic pathway (groundwater, not rivers), operating on a substrate (shallow clear-water lagoons) whose ecology is known to flip at low thresholds. What makes the pathway different from Pathways 2 or 3a is the near absence of buffering: no riverine dilution, no deep stratified basin, and only limited tidal exchange with the adjacent Atlantic through narrow inlets.
