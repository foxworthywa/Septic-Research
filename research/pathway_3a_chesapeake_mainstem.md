# Pathway 3a — Chesapeake Bay mainstem and ESVA Bayside tributaries

## Mechanism of harm

Failing onsite wastewater treatment systems (OWTS) on Virginia's Eastern Shore (ESVA) export nitrogen — and to a lesser but increasing extent phosphorus — to the Chesapeake Bay mainstem and its Bayside tributaries through two coupled delivery modes. First, the dominant mode on ESVA is diffuse submarine and sub-tidal groundwater discharge: partially treated effluent enters the shallow Columbia surficial aquifer as nitrified nitrate, travels along short (typically <2 km) and geochemically oxic flow paths, and is discharged directly through creek-bottom sediments and shoreline seeps into Pocomoke Sound, Tangier Sound, and the small tidal creeks that drain the western peninsula (Onancock, Pungoteague, Nassawadox, Cherrystone, Old Plantation) (Speiran 1996; Richardson 1994; Mitchell et al. 2021). Because riparian denitrification is spatially confined to thin carbon-rich strips, most shallow septic N bypasses the last-meter denitrification zone that the Chesapeake Bay Program (CBP) Phase 6 watershed model applies to "land-to-water" septic delivery (CBP 2017; Ator et al. 2020). Second, surface-water delivery occurs where failing systems back up into lawns, storm drains, or farm ditches that drain to the tidal creeks during heavy rainfall and flood-tide events, bypassing the subsurface treatment train altogether.

Once in tidal waters, this anthropogenic nitrogen drives the classical Chesapeake eutrophication cascade (Kemp et al. 2005; Boesch 2019). Elevated dissolved inorganic nitrogen (DIN) stimulates phytoplankton production in the euphotic surface layer; the resulting organic matter sinks, is remineralized by heterotrophic bacteria in the pycnocline and bottom water, and consumes dissolved oxygen faster than stratified summer circulation can resupply it, producing the seasonal "dead zone." Decades of monitoring have established a tight, quasi-linear relationship between January–May total nitrogen load to the Bay and summer hypoxic volume (Murphy et al. 2011; Frankel et al. 2022). A nontrivial share of the integrated N load is legacy groundwater nitrogen in transit — the groundwater reservoir attenuates with decadal lag — and septic systems, which release N directly to groundwater with no point-source controls, are a disproportionate contributor to that legacy stock relative to their nominal annual loading fraction (Sanford & Pope 2013; Ator et al. 2020).

The cascade has several symptoms relevant to ESVA Bayside waters. (1) Chlorophyll-a and primary productivity rise with DIN, especially in shallow, well-mixed tidal creeks where light is rarely limiting. (2) HAB species — notably the mixotrophic dinoflagellates *Prorocentrum minimum* (mahogany tide) and *Karlodinium veneficum*, and in low-salinity reaches *Microcystis aeruginosa* — are promoted by elevated DIN/DIP ratios and organic-N forms characteristic of wastewater (Glibert et al. 2001, 2014; Heil et al. 2005); *Karlodinium* blooms have caused documented fish kills in Bay tributaries via karlotoxins. (3) Shading from phytoplankton and epiphytes drives the decline of submerged aquatic vegetation (SAV) — *Zostera marina* and *Ruppia maritima* — with cascading consequences for juvenile blue crabs, scallops, and waterfowl (Orth et al. 2017; Lefcheck et al. 2018; Hensel et al. 2023). (4) Hypoxia enhances sediment P release, closing a positive feedback that delays recovery (Testa et al. 2017). (5) Low DO and sulfide stress oyster reefs and blue-crab nursery habitat (Pathways 4, 5).

Phosphorus contributions from ESVA septic systems to the Bay mainstem are quantitatively smaller than N but not negligible. Sandy, low-Fe/Al Columbia aquifer soils offer finite P-sorption capacity; aged drainfields can reach P breakthrough and deliver dissolved reactive P via groundwater (Robertson et al. 1998; Lusk et al. 2017). Because the Chesapeake's mesohaline reaches are seasonally P-limited in spring, even modest P additions from failing systems can shift bloom phenology and composition (Malone et al. 1996; Harding et al. 2016).

## Resolution of the 6% vs. 3.3% Bay-nitrogen-from-septic figure

Both figures are correct — they refer to different accounting frames, model versions, and geographic scales, and the apparent contradiction dissolves once those are specified.

**The ~6% figure** traces to the Phase 5.3 Chesapeake Bay Watershed Model that supported the 2010 Bay TMDL (USEPA 2010). In the Phase 5.3 accounting, the Bay watershed edge-of-tide nitrogen load was estimated at roughly 300 million lb yr⁻¹ (2009 base condition), and septic systems accounted for approximately 15–17 million lb yr⁻¹, or about 5–6% of the total delivered TN load. That is the number that circulated in CBP fact sheets and regional reporting during the TMDL roll-out era (2010–2014) and that the project proposal appears to cite.

**The ~3.3% figure** comes from the current Phase 6 model as implemented in the Chesapeake Assessment Scenario Tool (CAST v2019–v2023), which uses a 2018 (and more recently 2022) base calibration (CBP 2017; CAST documentation). Phase 6 recalibrated septic loading methods in several ways that moved the number downward: (a) it incorporates revised per-capita septic N production and updated drainfield soil attenuation factors developed in the STAC septic expert panel process; (b) it assumes ~60% of septic houses are connected to conventional drainfields with 60% attenuation on land-to-water delivery plus a Bay-delivery factor <1; and (c) the total edge-of-tide TN load estimate itself rose modestly in Phase 6 due to revised atmospheric, agricultural, and stormwater accounting, shrinking septic's denominator share. In CAST the septic source sector now reports roughly 8–10 million lb yr⁻¹ TN delivered to the Bay, or ~3.0–3.4% of the Phase 6 TN delivered load of ~260 million lb yr⁻¹. Septic is excluded from Phase 6 P accounting.

**Neither number captures the ESVA reality.** Both are Bay-watershed averages dominated by the Susquehanna and Potomac basins, where point sources and agricultural runoff overwhelm any local septic signal. For ESVA — very high unsewered fraction (≈80–90% of households; Mitchell et al. 2021; VA Wastewater Infrastructure Working Group 2021), sandy oxic aquifers with weak denitrification, and short groundwater-to-tide flow paths — the local percentage is far higher. Calculations in Mitchell et al. (2021) plus CBP sub-basin loadings suggest septic can account for 20–40% of delivered TN in small ESVA Bayside catchments. STAC and independent reviews (Ator et al. 2020; Sanford & Pope 2013) further note that Phase 6 applies a uniformly high land-to-water denitrification attenuation that likely overestimates removal on sandy coastal-plain soils and does not explicitly represent decadal groundwater legacy, so the Phase 6 septic figure is a lower bound for ESVA.

Bottom line: the proposal should cite the Phase 6 Bay-wide figure (~3.3%) as the current official CBP estimate, note that the older ~6% figure was from the Phase 5.3 TMDL basis, and emphasize that both are Bay-wide averages that materially understate the local ESVA contribution where failing septic is concentrated, hydrogeologically efficient at delivering N to tidal waters, and the dominant unsewered wastewater vector.

## ESVA-specific evidence

Direct, attribution-grade ESVA Bayside evidence linking failing septic to mainstem or tributary ecological harm is thinner than the groundwater/creek nearshore evidence base in Pathways 1 and 2, but several converging lines are relevant.

*Failure rates and vulnerability.* Mitchell, Isdell, Herman & Tombleson (2021, Frontiers Mar Sci 8:631757) combined parcel-level septic locations, soil drainage, depth-to-water-table, and SLR projections across Virginia's coastal plain and found ESVA (Accomack and Northampton) has among the highest at-risk densities in the Commonwealth. Under a 1.5 ft SLR scenario, a large fraction of Bayside and seaside drainfields would fall within 1 m of the seasonal high water table, eliminating the unsaturated treatment zone and converting nominally functioning systems into de facto direct discharges. Northampton's Bayside shoreline (Cherrystone, Kings, Hungars, Old Plantation Creeks) already shows this transition. The CBSAG companion report and the 2021 Virginia Wastewater Infrastructure Working Group report (RD883) formalized these findings for policy.

*Hydrogeologic susceptibility.* USGS studies of the Eastern Shore surficial aquifer (Richardson 1994; Speiran 1996) documented oxic, sandy flow paths from upland recharge areas to small tidal creek margins, with groundwater travel times of years to a few decades and limited denitrification outside narrow riparian zones. Bratton et al. (2009) measured elevated DIN concentrations in submarine groundwater discharge along the Bayside shoreline, and Ator et al. (2020) used USGS SPARROW and Phase 6 results to show that Eastern Shore sub-basins have among the highest unit-area septic-N delivery ratios in the Bay watershed.

*Water-quality condition of ESVA Bayside tributaries.* Virginia DEQ water-quality assessments and CBP Bay Barometer reporting consistently identify Pocomoke Sound, Tangier Sound, Onancock Creek, Pungoteague Creek, Nassawadox Creek, Cherrystone Inlet, and Old Plantation Creek as impaired or partially impaired for chlorophyll-a, DO, and/or bacteria. The NOAA ASSETS/NEEA assessment (Bricker et al. 2008) ranked Pocomoke Sound as having "high" overall eutrophic condition with elevated chlorophyll-a and episodic HABs; Tangier Sound as "moderate-high." Both are strongly influenced by nonpoint loadings that include unsewered residential inputs.

*SAV status on the Bayside.* VIMS annual SAV aerial surveys (Orth et al. 2017; Lefcheck et al. 2018) show that Bayside ESVA segments — particularly Pocomoke Sound (CB7PH, POCMH) and the Tangier/Pungoteague reach — carry among the lowest eelgrass densities in the Virginia mainstem Bay, and experienced severe losses during the 2005 and 2010 marine heat-wave events from which they have recovered only partially. While temperature stress is the proximate driver, nutrient- and turbidity-mediated light limitation sets the baseline tolerance threshold, and localized septic N contributions to Bayside creek mouths plausibly contribute to that stress. Lefcheck et al. (2018) documented Bay-wide SAV recovery tracking TN reductions since 1984 but noted lagging recovery in sub-segments with continuing nonpoint pressure — a category that includes ESVA Bayside creeks.

*HABs.* *Prorocentrum minimum* mahogany tides are recorded in Pocomoke Sound and the lower Bay off ESVA (Glibert et al. 2001, 2014). The *Pfiesteria* and *Karlodinium* fish-kill events of the late 1990s on the Pocomoke were interpreted as nutrient-driven (Burkholder & Glasgow 2001); while the dominant N source is agricultural, septic contributions from unsewered Virginia-side shoreline communities are part of the cumulative load. *Microcystis aeruginosa* occasionally blooms in low-salinity Bayside ponds and ditches but is not dominant in the mainstem tributaries.

*Aggregate load framing.* Capps et al. (2020 ES&T) identified Delmarva — ESVA included — as a national OWTS risk hotspot where high OWTS density, aging infrastructure, sandy soils, and shallow water tables combine to produce outsized per-capita N delivery to adjacent estuaries, consistent with Mitchell et al. (2021) and with the Phase 6 Bay-wide figure being an underestimate for ESVA.

## Analogue and Bay-wide comparison evidence

Because ESVA-specific attribution is limited, the case for Pathway 3a rests partly on analogue evidence from other unsewered, low-relief Bayside and Atlantic-coast-plain systems.

*Bay mainstem eutrophication-recovery evidence.* The Kemp et al. (2005) synthesis and Testa et al. (2017, 2018) updates established the quantitative framework linking Bay TN load to hypoxic volume, chlorophyll-a, and SAV decline. Boesch (2019) and Frankel et al. (2022) quantified decade-scale improvements in dissolved oxygen and chlorophyll in the mainstem driven by point-source wastewater upgrades and some agricultural best-management-practice implementation — while noting that nonpoint sources, including septic, are the residual portion hardest to reduce. The Lefcheck et al. (2018) PNAS paper is the strongest direct evidence that Bay-wide TN reduction causes measurable SAV recovery; it documented a 23% decline in TN since 1984 coinciding with a 316% increase in SAV cover (7,878 → 24,874 ha), with a Cozzarelli-Prize-recognized structural equation model linking land use to nutrient load to SAV response, and identifying nonpoint sources as the more influential lever compared to point-source sewage. This is the scientific basis for treating residual septic N as a meaningful SAV stressor in ESVA Bayside reaches where loads have not yet come down.

*Septic → seagrass analogues.* Cape Cod studies (Valiela et al. 1990, 1997, 2016; Howes et al. 2003) documented dose-response relationships between septic groundwater N and eelgrass loss, macroalgal accumulation, and hypoxia in shallow systems with sandy aquifers and short flow paths — hydrogeologically close analogues to ESVA Bayside. On Long Island, Kinney & Valiela (2011) and Gobler et al. (2005) linked unsewered density to N loading, brown tide, and seagrass decline. In Indian River Lagoon, Lapointe et al. (2015, 2020) used δ¹⁵N and sucralose tracers to attribute seagrass loss and macroalgal blooms to OWTS. Collectively, where unsewered density is high, sandy aquifers dominate, and flushing is limited, septic N drives eutrophication-cascade symptoms at the sub-estuary scale — conditions met by ESVA Bayside tributaries.

*Upper Bay and Potomac septic-load studies.* On the Maryland side of the Bay, Lindsey et al. (2003) and Ator et al. (2020) used groundwater age-dating and SPARROW decomposition to identify septic contributions to baseflow nitrate in Delmarva, Patuxent, and lower Potomac sub-basins. Shields et al. (2008) estimated that ~9% of the nitrogen reaching the Patuxent originates from septic. These sub-basin figures bracket the ESVA expectation and support that the Phase 6 Bay-average 3.3% substantially understates septic contributions in any watershed where unsewered homes concentrate near tidal water.

*HAB literature.* Glibert et al. (2001, 2014) and Heil, Glibert & Fan (2005) tied the rise of *Prorocentrum minimum* and *Karlodinium veneficum* in the Chesapeake to changes in dissolved organic N and the N:P ratio — both shifted by septic effluent relative to stoichiometrically-balanced agricultural runoff. The Bay-wide HAB record therefore pulls in the same direction as the nutrient-loading literature.

## Strength-of-evidence assessment

*Mechanism (strong).* The biogeochemistry linking septic effluent → groundwater nitrate → tidal-water DIN → eutrophication cascade is textbook and supported by decades of Chesapeake-specific measurement (Kemp et al. 2005; Testa et al. 2017; Murphy et al. 2011).

*Magnitude of ESVA septic contribution to the Bay mainstem (moderate, high uncertainty).* CBP Phase 6 gives a Bay-wide 3.0–3.4% septic share, and the older Phase 5.3 / TMDL-era figure was ~5–6%. Both bracket a true value that is almost certainly higher for ESVA itself. Mitchell et al. (2021), Ator et al. (2020), and the Wastewater Infrastructure Working Group report agree on the qualitative "disproportionate for ESVA" framing, but there is no published basin-specific mass-balance number for ESVA septic N delivery to the Bay mainstem with defensible uncertainty bounds.

*Linkage to specific ecological outcomes on ESVA Bayside waters (moderate).* Lefcheck et al. (2018) provides Bay-wide evidence that TN drives SAV recovery; Bricker et al. (2008) and CBP Bay Barometer reports score Pocomoke Sound as eutrophic and VA DEQ lists several Bayside creeks as impaired for chl-a and DO. These outcomes are plausibly and partly septic-attributable but are dominated by agricultural and atmospheric loads at the ecosystem scale. HAB linkages (*P. minimum*, *K. veneficum*) are mechanism-plausible and observed in adjacent waters but not quantitatively attributed to ESVA septic.

*Analogue evidence (strong).* Cape Cod, Long Island, and Indian River Lagoon studies provide dose-response evidence for seagrass and eutrophication harm from septic N in hydrogeologic analogues. Transferring those findings to ESVA is well justified.

*Integrated assessment.* The case that failing ESVA septic systems contribute to eutrophication, SAV stress, and HAB conditions in the Bay mainstem and Bayside tributaries is strong on mechanism, moderate on site-specific attribution, and strong on analogue. It is weakest where it would need to be strongest for policy: a validated basin-scale mass-balance that partitions observed chl-a, DO, or SAV cover changes in Pocomoke Sound, Tangier Sound, Onancock, Pungoteague, Nassawadox, Cherrystone, and Old Plantation among septic, agricultural, atmospheric, and legacy groundwater sources.

## Data gaps

1. **No sub-basin septic N mass balance for ESVA Bayside creeks.** The most important gap. CBP Phase 6 / CAST reports septic loads at the segment level but with uniform attenuation coefficients not calibrated for ESVA Columbia aquifer conditions. A properly constrained mass balance for Onancock, Pungoteague, Nassawadox, Cherrystone, and Old Plantation — using parcel-level septic locations, observed groundwater nitrate, and creek-mouth flux measurements — does not exist in the peer-reviewed literature.

2. **Missing direct septic-to-Bay submarine groundwater discharge flux measurements on the Bayside.** Bratton et al. (2009) provided a reconnaissance; there is no comprehensive, multi-season, shoreline-scale SGD flux study for ESVA Bayside comparable to what exists for Waquoit Bay or Indian River Lagoon. Radium-isotope and radon-222 end-member measurements would directly quantify septic delivery but have not been done at the density needed.

3. **Site-specific SAV attribution.** VIMS SAV aerial surveys record presence/absence and cover for Pocomoke Sound and Tangier Sound but do not partition the nutrient stressors driving observed declines. Water-column TN data at creek mouths are sparse relative to what would be needed for sub-segment attribution.

4. **HAB source attribution on Bayside.** Glibert et al. documented *P. minimum* and *K. veneficum* in lower Bay waters but did not publish source-tracking (δ¹⁵N, PPCP tracers) that would distinguish septic from agricultural N in bloom initiation areas on ESVA Bayside.

5. **Phosphorus breakthrough timing.** Phase 6 excludes septic P. The question of whether ESVA drainfields on sandy soils have reached P breakthrough — and what that adds to the Bayside tributary P load — is unanswered at the basin scale.

6. **Legacy groundwater N inventory.** Sanford & Pope (2013) estimated decadal groundwater N travel times Bay-wide; an ESVA-specific groundwater N inventory quantifying how much legacy septic N is stored in the Columbia aquifer and over what timeline it will discharge would change the policy framing materially.

7. **Climate-change coupling.** Mitchell et al. (2021) mapped SLR-driven septic failure risk, but the coupled question — how rising water tables plus warming-driven hypoxia sensitivity will interact — lacks a quantitative projection for ESVA Bayside specifically.

8. **Failure-rate ground truth.** Parcel-level failure rates for ESVA are still estimated from soil/water-table proxies rather than direct inspection or bacterial tracer surveys; the Virginia Department of Health pump-out data are not systematically compiled or published.
